The Economics Sequence

An ordered progression through microeconomics and macroeconomics, in curriculum order. Each article builds on the machinery of the last, connected by a two-sentence bridge explaining what the previous article established and what the next one needs.

Introduction

Trade lets two economies both gain whenever their opportunity costs differ. Where the price lands between those costs, and so who gets the gains, is set by how big each economy is.

Part 01 Principles

Comparative advantage

Two economies can both gain from trade at any price between their opportunity costs, but nobody gets to pick the price. An economy that's better at everything, trading with a partner its own size, can come away with nothing at all.

Bridge

Comparative advantage took each economy's techniques as given. Economic rent asks where a better technique comes from, and follows the reward a firm earns for being different until competition takes it away and spreads the idea.

Part 02 Mi2

Economic rent

Seven technologies can do the same job, and the rectangle test from first courses leaves a sensible middle option standing that isn't the cheapest at any price. Along the way we meet economic rent, and see why the competition that removes it is also what spreads a new idea.

September 2026Read →
Bridge

Economic rent followed one firm's margin as competition closed it. Supply and demand steps up to the whole market, and shows that a line through its prices and quantities is a mix of the two curves rather than the demand curve.

Part 03 Mi3

Supply and demand

A line through a market's prices and quantities is a mix of demand and supply, not the demand curve. The R² that says how well the line fits also says how little the data can tell us about demand.

September 2026Read →
Bridge

Supply and demand took the slopes of the curves as given. Elasticity looks at how strongly quantity responds to price, and finds that the answer is a ratio of two lengths that each point cuts from its own tangent.

Part 04 Mi4

Elasticity

Elasticity is a ratio of two lengths that each point on a demand curve cuts from its own tangent. The picture works on any smooth curve, and it shows what the usual two-point formulas assume without saying so.

September 2026Read →
Bridge

Elasticity measured how responsive buyers are along a curve. Surplus and efficiency measures what the gains from trade are worth, and shows that a price ceiling which rations at random loses more than the familiar triangle counts.

Part 05 Mi5

Surplus and efficiency

The deadweight loss triangle counts the trades that don't happen. A price ceiling that rations at random adds a second loss, and in a linear market the two come to the size of the cut: cut output by 27% and 27% of the gains from trade are gone.

September 2026Read →
Bridge

Surplus and efficiency measured the gains from trade and what a cut in output loses. Tax incidence puts a tax between the price buyers pay and the price sellers get, and asks who really pays it.

Part 06 Mi6

Tax incidence

Collecting a tax from buyers or from sellers doesn't change who ends up paying it, and the elasticity rule for the split is exact only in a straight-line market. At the rate that raises the most money, half the trades are gone and the lost surplus is half the revenue.

September 2026Read →
Bridge

Tax incidence took the supply curve as given. Cost curves opens up the firm behind it, and shows that the plant built for an output is run at its own lowest cost per unit at only one output.

Part 07 Mi7

Cost curves

A firm builds the cheapest plant for the output it plans, and yet that plant is almost never run at its own lowest cost per unit. The envelope picture shows why, and why getting the plant a little wrong costs so little.

September 2026Read →
Bridge

Cost curves looked at one firm choosing its plant. Perfect competition lets firms like it into a market one at a time, and shows that because firms come in whole numbers, the long-run supply curve is a sawtooth rather than a flat line.

Part 08 Mi8

Perfect competition

Free entry is meant to push the price down to the lowest average cost. Because firms come in whole numbers, entry stops a little short, the firms that are in keep a small profit, and the long-run supply curve becomes a sawtooth.

September 2026Read →
Bridge

Perfect competition assumed firms that take the price as given. Markup and elasticity looks at a firm that sets its own price, and shows that the rule linking its markup to the elasticity of demand describes a single point.

Part 09 Mi9

Markup and elasticity

The best markup is one over the elasticity of demand, on every demand curve. But the rule describes a single point, and two curves that agree there can pass on a rise in cost very differently: half of it on one, two and a half times it on the other.

September 2026Read →
Bridge

Markup and elasticity looked at one price-setting firm on its own, and perfect competition at many identical ones. Monopolistic competition sits between them, with firms that each sell a slightly different product, and asks how many varieties a market ends up with.

Part 10 Mi10

Monopolistic competition

How many varieties does a market end up with when every firm sells something slightly different? With a finite number of firms, each familiar answer picks up a small correction, and every correction fades as one over the number of firms.

September 2026Read →
Bridge

Monopolistic competition had many firms, each too small to matter much to the others. Cartels and the prisoner's dilemma looks at a market with only a few, where firms try to act together, and shows why the firms that stay out gain the most.

Part 11 Mi11

Cartels and the prisoner's dilemma

When a few firms agree to make less, the firms that stay out gain more than the members do. In a simple model of competition, a cartel pays its members only if it takes in almost the whole market, and every member would rather be the firm outside.

September 2026Read →
Bridge

Cartels showed how one firm's output spills over onto its rivals. Tragedy of the commons finds the same kind of spillover on a shared resource, where each user counts the average catch rather than what its extra effort adds, and the resource's rent drains away.

Part 12 Mi12

Tragedy of the commons

On a lake anyone can fish, each boat counts the average catch rather than what its extra hours add, so the lake's rent drains away. Most of it is gone once a few boats share the lake, and the second boat does the most damage.

September 2026Read →
Bridge

Tragedy of the commons followed how much value a shared resource loses. Gini and the Lorenz curve closes the microeconomics principles by asking how income is shared out, and what a single number can and can't say about it.

Part 13 Mi13

Gini and the Lorenz curve

Two societies can share a Gini while their poorest live very differently, and two credit scorecards can share one while one catches 40% of defaulters in the riskiest tenth of applicants and the other 25.5%. One number can't say where the inequality sits.

September 2026Read →
Bridge

Gini and the Lorenz curve looked at how income is shared out. Measuring GDP opens the macroeconomics principles by asking how much there is to share, and shows that the minus sign in front of imports is a correction rather than a cost.

Part 14 Ma1

Measuring GDP

The minus sign in front of imports in C + I + G + X − M is a correction, not a cost. An import changes GDP only through the home production it replaces, and the trade line in a GDP release reads the same whether that's none of it or all of it.

September 2026Read →
Bridge

Measuring GDP added up a year's production at that year's prices. Price indices asks how to compare prices across years, and finds that a fixed basket overstates the rise in the cost of living by an amount that depends on how readily people switch between goods.

Part 15 Ma2

Price indices

A fixed basket overstates the cost of living by about half of σ times the variance of the price changes, where σ is how readily people switch between goods. Averaging last year's basket with this year's gets close to the true answer without ever knowing σ.

September 2026Read →
Bridge

Price indices showed how a single number can hide the switching behind it. Unemployment flows does the same for the unemployment rate, a stock fed by people losing jobs and people finding them, which settles where the two flows balance.

Part 16 Ma3

Unemployment flows

The unemployment rate settles where job losses and job finding balance, and that depends only on the ratio of the two. So a hiring freeze can nearly double unemployment while layoffs fall, and two towns at 6% can have spells averaging two months in one and eleven in the other.

September 2026Read →
Bridge

Unemployment flows looked at how an economy uses the workers it has from month to month. Compound growth turns to the long run, where a one-point gap in growth rates doubles the gap in living standards in about seventy years, and asks what happens when growth is uncertain.

Part 17 Ma4

Compound growth

A one-point gap in growth rates doubles the gap in living standards in about seventy years. But an economy whose growth swings grows more slowly than its average rate, and once growth is uncertain, the typical path falls behind the expected one for ever.

September 2026Read →
Bridge

Compound growth dealt with real output over decades. Money creation turns to the money that output is bought with, following one loan through three banks' balance sheets to show where deposits come from and what the money multiplier really measures.

Part 18 Ma5

Money creation

A bank that lends alone loses most of the loan in reserves, which is why bankers say deposits come first. Banks that lend in step lose none, so the system's loans create its deposits, and the money multiplier is a ratio that holds whichever way the causation runs.

September 2026Read →
Bridge

Money creation closed the macroeconomics principles with the banking system as a whole. Constrained choice opens intermediate microeconomics by stepping down to one person's decision, where an indifference curve meets a budget line, and asks what a pay rise does to how much someone chooses to work.

Part 19 U3

Constrained choice

Many first courses say theory can't tell us whether a pay rise makes people work more or less, and in general that's true. But for the Cobb–Douglas utility they usually draw, the income and substitution effects cancel completely, and hours don't move at any wage.

September 2026Read →
Bridge

Constrained choice looked at one person choosing against a fixed budget. Nash equilibrium brings in a second decision maker whose choice depends on ours, and shows what an equilibrium looks like when no cell of the payoff matrix is one.

Part 20 U4

Nash equilibrium

In one two-by-two game in eight, checking cells finds no equilibrium, and in those games each player's behaviour is set by the other player's payoffs. So in our model, a bigger fine buys less monitoring and leaves breaches where they were.

September 2026Read →
Bridge

Nash equilibrium looked at games where both players move at once. Bargaining and the surplus looks at two sides taking turns to make offers, and shows that how impatient each one is picks a single split of the gains.

Part 21 U5

Bargaining and the surplus

Every split on the frontier beats no deal, and the picture can't pick one. Once the two sides take turns making offers, the model settles on a single split, in which your share grows with the other side's impatience, and an outside option counts only once it beats that share.

September 2026Read →
Bridge

Bargaining and the surplus showed how two sides who can negotiate split the gains from a deal. Externalities brings in a party who isn't at the table, and compares a tax, a quota and a bargain as ways to deal with a cost that nobody prices.

Part 22 U10

Externalities

A tax, a quota and a bargain can bring a polluter down to the same output and move the same money into different pockets. Once the regulator is unsure of the costs, the tax does better when damage is flatter than benefit, and the quota when it's steeper.

September 2026Read →
Bridge

Externalities assumed the costly action could be seen and measured. Efficiency wages turns to one that can't be, a worker's effort, and shows why a firm might pay more than it has to and leave a queue of people outside.

Part 23 U6

Efficiency wages

When a wage buys effort, the cheapest effort sits where a 1% pay rise buys 1% more effort. That wage comes from the effort curve alone, so a firm hiring one worker or a thousand pays the same, and the queue outside is part of how it works.

The Finance Sequence

The technical side of markets, in reading order: how a trade happens, how a share is valued, and how portfolios and risk are measured. Like the economics sequence, each article is joined to the last by a two-sentence bridge.

Introduction

The finance sequence starts where every trade starts, at the queue of orders waiting on an exchange. The order book asks what a market order really pays, and why the shape of that queue matters.

Part 01 Fm1

The order book

A market order's average price sits a fixed share of the way from the best quote to the worst price it reaches: half in a flat book, two thirds when depth grows linearly. The shape also decides whether cost grows with size or its square root.

September 2026Read →
Bridge

The order book showed what it costs to get into a position. Volatility drag asks what happens once we hold one, and why a fund that resets its leverage every day drifts away from a multiple of the index.

Part 02 Fr1

Volatility drag

Over a year, a daily-reset fund's result depends on two numbers, the index's return and how much it moved along the way. A flat year at 20% volatility costs a 3× fund about 11% and a −3× fund about 21%.

September 2026Read →
Bridge

Volatility drag was about how returns add up over time. The dividend discount model steps back to ask what a share is worth in the first place, and why one gap sets both its price and its sensitivity to rates.

Part 03 Fm7

The dividend discount model

Priced as a growing stream of dividends, a share depends on one gap, the discount rate minus the growth rate. That gap is the dividend yield and the inverse of the duration, so at a 3% gap a one-point rise in rates takes a quarter off the price.

September 2026Read →
Bridge

The dividend discount model priced a single share. Diversification puts many shares together and asks what holding more of them buys us, in risk and in growth.

Part 04 Fr3

Diversification

Holding more stocks shrinks risk towards a floor set by how they move together, and ten stocks remove 90% of what can go. It also raises the growth rate by half the variance removed, so over thirty years only one stock in six beats the portfolio.

September 2026Read →
Bridge

Diversification used equal weights because they were simple. Mean-variance optimisation asks whether the textbook's best weights do better once their inputs have to be estimated from history.

Part 05 Fr4

Mean-variance optimisation

With estimated average returns, Markowitz's optimiser earns the best Sharpe ratio times the cosine of an angle set by the number of assets and the length of the history. To beat equal weights with ten assets, it needs about 68 years of data.

September 2026Read →
Bridge

Mean-variance optimisation looked at a single period. Time diversification asks what happens to the risk of stocks as we hold them for longer, and why lower odds of losing aren't the same as smaller losses.

Part 06 Fr16

Time diversification

Holding stocks for longer lowers the chance that they end behind bonds, from 42% over one year to 14% over thirty. But the average shortfall when they do grows from 13% to 37%, and a 1-in-20 bad outcome keeps getting worse for 17 years.

September 2026Read →
Bridge

Time diversification showed that holding for longer lowers the odds of a shortfall and deepens it. Samuelson's 1963 paper asks whether repeating a bet we'd refuse once can ever make it worth taking.

Part 07 Fr17

Samuelson’s fallacy of large numbers

If we'd refuse a bet at every level of wealth we could reach, we should refuse a hundred of them too. For someone with constant risk aversion, a hundred bets are worth a hundred times one, so the answer never flips.

September 2026Read →
Bridge

Samuelson argued that repeating a risk doesn't shrink it. Bodie's 1995 paper puts a price on long-run risk by asking what it costs to insure stocks against ending behind bonds.

Part 08 Fr18

Bodie’s shortfall insurance

Insuring stocks against ending behind bonds costs about 8% of the money for one year and 42% for thirty, even as the chance of needing it falls. The price depends only on volatility and time, never on the premium stocks are expected to earn.

September 2026Read →
