COMPARATIVE ADVANTAGE

Two economies both gain from trade at any price between their opportunity costs, even when one of them is better at everything. But a market doesn't let anyone pick the price, and as we'll see, an economy that's better at everything, trading with a partner its own size, can come away with exactly nothing.

Martin Le, September 2026

Let's imagine two small economies that make only two things: tools and grain. In the Valley, one worker can make 9 tools in a day, or grow 18 sacks of grain instead. On the Coast, where the soil is thinner and the workshops are older, a worker can make 2 tools or grow 9 sacks. So the Valley is better at both, and by a wide margin.

Most first courses in economics have a lesson about a pair like this, and it's one of the most useful ideas in the subject: the two economies should still trade, and both of them will come out ahead. The lesson comes with a two-step procedure for showing it, and it's worth running that procedure before we start poking at it. The first step is deciding who should make what.

What one worker makes in a day, working on one good

The Valley is better at both. So who should grow the grain?

Pick one. There's no trick in the numbers.

What a tool really costs

The number that matters is what each worker gives up. A Valley worker who spends time making a tool loses the 2 sacks of grain they could have grown instead, while a Coast worker loses 4.5 sacks. Economists call that sacrifice the opportunity cost of a tool, and it's the only thing this decision depends on. Turned around, a sack of grain costs a Valley worker half a tool and a Coast worker only two-ninths of one.

So the Valley has an absolute advantage in both goods, which means it makes more of each per worker, but a comparative advantage, which means a lower opportunity cost, only in tools. The Coast has the comparative advantage in grain, even though it grows less of it. If the Valley makes the tools and the Coast grows the grain, each good is made by the economy that gives up the least to make it.

We can draw each economy's choices as a straight line. A Valley worker can end the day anywhere between 18 sacks with no tools and 9 tools with no sacks, and the line joining those two points is called the production possibility frontier. Its slope is the opportunity cost, which is why the Valley's frontier is flatter than the Coast's in the figure below. Both are drawn on the same scale, so the Coast's is smaller as well.

Any price in between

The second step is the price. Suppose the Valley makes only tools, the Coast grows only grain, and they swap some of each. The Valley won't sell a tool for fewer than 2 sacks, because it could grow 2 sacks with the same time. The Coast won't pay more than 4.5 sacks, because that's what a tool costs it to make. So a deal can only happen at a price between the two opportunity costs.

To keep score, we'll assume that everybody spends half of their income on each good, which we'll stick with for the whole article, and we'll measure a worker's basket by the geometric mean of their tools and their sacks, which is the natural score when spending is split evenly. At the two ends of the range, something specific happens. At exactly 2 sacks per tool, the Valley's dashed line lies on top of its own frontier, so trade offers it nothing it couldn't already make, and its gain is exactly zero. At exactly 4.5, the same is true of the Coast. That's the textbook result, and so far it's airtight: pick any price strictly between the two opportunity costs, and both economies gain, even though one of them is better at everything.

There's only one thing missing, which is that nobody gets to pick the price.

Let the market choose

In a real economy, thousands of workers and buyers trade at whatever price clears the market, so the price is an outcome rather than a choice. So let's give each economy 1,000 workers, open the border between them, and see where the price settles.

1,000 workers in each economy, spending half their income on each good

Without trade, the two economies make 5,500 tools and 13,500 sacks a day between them.

The price settles at 2 sacks per tool, which is the Valley's own opportunity cost. As we saw a moment ago, that's the one price at which the Valley gains nothing, and indeed a Valley worker ends up with 4.5 tools and 9 sacks, which is exactly the basket they had before. The Coast, on the other hand, gains 50%, which is the most it could gain at any price in the range. So the economy that's better at everything, trading with a partner of the same size, gets none of the gain.

This isn't a quirk of our numbers. In a two-good world with spending split evenly, it happens to every pair of equal-sized economies in which one is better at both goods. The checks behind this page try 100,000 random pairs like that, and the more productive economy gains exactly nothing in every one of them.

Why the price sticks at 2

The reason is capacity. At any price above 2 sacks, every Valley worker would rather make tools, so the world's grain would have to come from the Coast alone. But the Coast can grow at most 9,000 sacks a day, and at those prices buyers want a good deal more grain than that. So some Valley workers have to keep farming, and they'll only do it if a tool sells for no more than the 2 sacks they could grow instead. In other words, an economy that keeps making both goods pins the price at its own opportunity cost.

A test for who gains

There's a simple test for which of those cases you're in, and it compares whole economies rather than single workers. With spending split evenly, the Valley gains exactly when the Coast can grow more grain in total than the Valley could, and the Coast gains exactly when the Valley can make more tools in total than the Coast could. Put together, both economies gain exactly when each one is the bigger producer of the good it sells.

With 1,000 workers each, the Valley could make 9,000 tools a day against the Coast's 2,000, so that half of the test passes and the Coast gains. But the Coast could grow only 9,000 sacks against the Valley's 18,000, so the other half fails, and the Valley gains nothing. The checks behind this page run the test on 100,000 random pairs of economies of all sizes, and it agrees with the full market calculation every time.

How wide is the band?

If we plot the market price against the size ratio on logarithmic axes, we get a straight line with a slope of exactly 1 and a flat stretch at each end. With our numbers the middle part is especially simple, because a Valley worker's 9 tools and a Coast worker's 9 sacks cancel, which leaves the price inside the band equal to the size ratio itself. The flat stretches sit at the two opportunity costs, so the band runs from 2 to 4.5, a factor of exactly 2.25, which is the ratio of the two opportunity costs.

What absolute advantage does decide

None of this makes the Valley's productivity irrelevant, because it decides wages. A tool costs a Valley wage divided by 9, and a sack costs a Coast wage divided by 9, so with our numbers a Valley wage is worth exactly as many Coast wages as a tool is worth sacks. That keeps a Valley wage between 2 and 4.5 Coast wages, which is between the Valley's productivity edge in grain and its edge in tools. At equal size it's exactly twice a Coast wage, because both economies are still growing grain, and at grain the Valley is exactly twice as productive. You can watch the wage move with size in the lab above.

This is also the honest answer to a common worry about trading with a poorer economy. The Coast can sell grain to the Valley only because its wages are lower, and its wages are lower because its workers are less productive, not because trade made them so. Trade doesn't equalise wages in this model, and it doesn't leave anybody worse off either.

When the Coast catches up

Here's a question with a less comfortable answer. Suppose the Coast gets better at something. Is that good news for the Valley? It sounds as if it should be, since the Valley buys from the Coast, but Paul Samuelson argued in 2004 that it depends on which good the Coast gets better at, and this model shows why. Let's hold the Coast at three times the Valley's size, where both economies gain 22.5%, and raise its productivity in one good.

If the Coast gets better at grain, which is what the Valley buys, the Valley's gain rises all the way, reaching 73.2% once a Coast worker grows twice as much. But if the Coast gets better at tools, which is what the Valley sells, the Valley's gain doesn't move at all until a Coast worker can make 3 tools a day. That's the point where the Coast's total tool capacity matches the Valley's. From there the Valley's gain falls, and at 4.5 tools a day it's exactly zero, because the two economies now have the same opportunity cost and nothing to trade. Past that point the Coast has the comparative advantage in tools, trade starts again in the other direction, and the Valley gains once more, reaching 41.4% at 9 tools a day.

The Coast's own gain has a surprise in it too. Before the Valley notices anything, the Coast's gain from trade falls from 22.5% to exactly zero, even though a Coast worker's basket doesn't change at all: it stays at 4.5 sacks and 1.5 tools a day. What changes is the basket the same worker could make without trade, which catches up until, at 3 tools a day, trade adds nothing to it.

More goods, a wider band

It's fair to ask whether the exact zero survives in a world with more than two goods, and the answer is mostly no, which is good news for the textbook. Suppose there are N goods with spending split evenly between them, and that the Valley's productivity edge over the Coast is spread evenly, on a log scale, from 2 to 4.5 across them. Then the Valley gains nothing exactly when the Coast has at most 2 / (N − 1) workers for each of its own, and the Coast gains nothing exactly when it has 4.5 × (N − 1) or more. With two goods, that's our band from 2 to 4.5 again.

What this costs you

A model this small is useful because of what it leaves out, and a few of the omissions matter here.

First, there's only one kind of worker, so everybody inside an economy gains by the same amount. Real trade creates losers inside each country, and the models that show it, with land or machines that can't move between industries, tell a different story from this one. Second, workers switch between tools and grain instantly and at no cost, and nobody is ever out of work along the way. Third, there are no transport costs, which in reality open up a range of prices at which nobody trades at all. Fourth, everybody spends a fixed share of their income on each good, which is the case economists call Cobb–Douglas. The fixed pie and the unchanged grain output are properties of that case, and if the two goods were easier to swap for one another, the band would be wider.

And finally, there are only two economies. With three or more, even the first step of the procedure stops being reliable, because a way of dividing up production can pass every comparison between two economies and still waste output. In a quick experiment behind this page, that happened in about 12% of random worlds with three economies and three goods. The general test, due to Ronald Jones, looks for the assignment of goods to economies that makes the product of their labour requirements as small as possible, and comparing pairs can miss it.

So how often does the bad regime turn up? In a world of two goods, often, because the band is only as wide as the gap between the opportunity costs, while real economies differ enormously in size. In a world of many goods, much less often, since the exact zero needs a very small partner. What doesn't depend on the number of goods is the direction of the effect: as an economy shrinks relative to its partner, it trades at prices closer to the partner's costs, and its share of the gain grows.

Where this leaves the procedure

The procedure most courses teach is correct as far as it goes. Comparative advantage really does decide who makes what, and any price strictly between the two opportunity costs really does leave both economies better off. What the procedure quietly assumes is the part that decides who gains, namely that the price lands somewhere in the middle. In a market it lands wherever relative size puts it, and for an economy that's better at everything, trading with a partner of its own size, that's the one price at which trade gives it nothing at all.

That's a useful thing to carry into any argument about trade. The statement that trade is good for both sides is really a statement about a range of prices, so the question worth asking is always where in that range the actual price sits, and why.

The reason is capacity. Let's see what happens at any price above 2 sacks: every Valley worker would rather make tools, so the world's grain would have to come from the Coast alone. But the Coast can grow at most 9,000 sacks a day, and at those prices buyers want a good deal more grain than that. So some Valley workers have to keep farming, and they'll only do it if a tool sells for no more than the 2 sacks they could grow instead. In other words, an economy that keeps making both goods pins the price at its own opportunity cost.

A test for who gains

There's a simple test we can use to tell which of those cases we're in, and it compares whole economies rather than single workers. With spending split evenly, the Valley gains exactly when the Coast can grow more grain in total than the Valley could. The Coast gains exactly when the Valley can make more tools in total than the Coast could. Put together, both economies gain exactly when each one is the bigger producer of the good it sells.

With 1,000 workers each, the Valley could make 9,000 tools a day against the Coast's 2,000, so that half of the test passes and the Coast gains. But the Coast could grow only 9,000 sacks against the Valley's 18,000, so the other half fails, and the Valley gains nothing. If we run the test on random pairs of economies of all sizes, it agrees with the full market calculation every time.

How wide is the band?

If we plot the market price against the size ratio on logarithmic axes, we get a straight line with a slope of exactly 1 and a flat stretch at each end. With our numbers the middle part is especially simple, because a Valley worker's 9 tools and a Coast worker's 9 sacks cancel, which leaves the price inside the band equal to the size ratio itself. The flat stretches sit at the two opportunity costs, so the band runs from 2 to 4.5, a factor of exactly 2.25, which is the ratio of the two opportunity costs.

What absolute advantage does decide

None of this makes the Valley's productivity irrelevant, because it decides wages. A tool costs a Valley wage divided by 9, and a sack costs a Coast wage divided by 9. So with our numbers, a Valley wage is worth as many Coast wages as a tool is worth sacks. That keeps a Valley wage between 2 and 4.5 Coast wages, which is between the Valley's productivity edge in grain and its edge in tools. At equal size it's exactly twice a Coast wage, because both economies are still growing grain, and at grain the Valley is exactly twice as productive. You can watch the wage move with size in the lab above.

This also gives us an answer to a common worry about trading with a poorer economy. The Coast can sell grain to the Valley only because its wages are lower, and its wages are lower because its workers are less productive, not because trade made them so. Trade doesn't equalise wages in this model, and it doesn't leave anybody worse off either.

When the Coast catches up

Here's a question with a less comfortable answer. Suppose the Coast gets better at something. Is that good news for the Valley? It sounds as if it should be, since the Valley buys from the Coast. But Paul Samuelson argued in 2004 that it depends on which good the Coast gets better at, and our model shows why. More goods, a wider band

It's fair to ask whether the exact zero survives in a world with more than two goods, and the answer is mostly no, which is good news for the textbook. Then the Valley gains nothing exactly when the Coast has at most 2 / (N − 1) workers for each of its own, and the Coast gains nothing exactly when it has 4.5 × (N − 1) or more. What this costs you

Our model is useful because of what it leaves out, and a few of the omissions matter here.

First, there's only one kind of worker in our model, so everybody inside an economy gains by the same amount. Real trade creates losers inside each country, and the models that show it, with land or machines that can't move between industries, tell a different story from ours.

Second, our workers switch between tools and grain instantly and at no cost, and nobody is ever out of work along the way.

Third, we've left out transport costs, which in reality open up a range of prices at which nobody trades at all.

Fourth, everybody spends a fixed share of their income on each good, which is the case economists call Cobb–Douglas. The fixed pie and the unchanged grain output are properties of that case, and if the two goods were easier to swap for one another, the band would be wider.

And finally, we've only looked at two economies. With three or more, even the first step of the procedure stops being reliable, because a way of dividing up production can pass every comparison between two economies and still waste output. When we tried random worlds with three economies and three goods, that happened in about 12% of them. The general test, due to Ronald Jones, looks for the assignment of goods to economies that makes the product of their labour requirements as small as possible, and comparing pairs can miss it.

So how often should we expect the bad regime to turn up? In a world of two goods, often, because the band is only as wide as the gap between the opportunity costs, while real economies differ enormously in size. In a world of many goods, much less often, since the exact zero needs a very small partner. What doesn't depend on the number of goods is the direction of the effect. As an economy shrinks relative to its partner, it trades at prices closer to the partner's costs, and its share of the gain grows.

Where this leaves the procedure

The procedure we started with is correct as far as it goes. Comparative advantage really does decide who makes what, and any price strictly between the two opportunity costs really does leave both economies better off. What the procedure quietly assumes is the part that decides who gains, namely that the price lands somewhere in the middle. In a market it lands wherever relative size puts it, and for an economy that's better at everything, trading with a partner of its own size, that's the one price at which trade gives it nothing at all.

That's a useful thing for us to carry into any argument about trade. The statement that trade is good for both sides is really a statement about a range of prices, so the question worth asking is always where in that range the actual price sits, and why.

Why competition both kills the rent and spreads the idea

A rent like this attracts rivals. A rival who adopts Indexed can copy the pioneer's product, offer the same run a little cheaper, and keep the difference for as long as the undercut stays under the gap. What this diagram costs you

First, this is a partial-equilibrium story, which means we look at one market on its own and hold everything outside it fixed. There's one job, one month and two inputs, with no capacity constraints and no sunk costs. The rent here comes from a technology gap, while real rents also come from patents, licences, brands and network effects, and those last longer than one round of undercutting.

Second, we've measured the rent in machine-day-equivalents, which is honest but a little abstract. Turning it into money needs a price for a run, and in this model that price is doing the rent's own work.

And finally, competition here is a price war between recipes. The process that actually diffuses an innovation through an industry, with imitation after a lag, licensing and staff moving between firms, sits behind the envelope's movement rather than on top of it.

Where this leaves the rectangle test

The rectangle test tells us which recipes nothing beats, while the envelope tells us which ones anybody would actually choose. Rent lives in the gap between the two, and competition closes that gap from below while it copies the very idea that opened it.

A line fitted through a market's prices and quantities is almost never the demand curve. As we'll see, it's a mix of demand and supply, and the R² that seems to say how good the fit is also says, exactly, how little the data can tell us about demand.

Let's start with the most familiar picture in economics. A demand curve slopes down, and it shows how much buyers are willing to pay at each quantity. A supply curve slopes up, and it shows what it costs sellers to produce each quantity. Where the two curves cross, we get the price and quantity at which the market clears.

The picture is clean, but a real market doesn't hand us its curves. What we actually get is a record of prices and quantities, one point a month, scattered in a noisy cloud. The natural next step is to fit a straight line through that cloud with ordinary least squares (OLS), the standard line of best fit, and call it the demand curve.

As we'll see, that line is almost never the demand curve. It's a weighted mix of demand and supply, and the R² we'd normally read as a sign of a good fit, which measures how much of the scatter the line explains, turns out to measure exactly how badly the data pins down the demand curve.

How to break out of the bracket

So how do economists get out of this bracket? More data won't do it. A bigger sample only estimates the same mixed-up moments more precisely, and the 1/R² bracket stays exactly as wide.

What we need instead is an instrumental variable, which is an outside shock Z that shifts supply without affecting demand. If we compare the average price and quantity when the instrument is high with when it's low, the slope between those two points comes only from supply moving, so it traces out the demand curve. That's what the figure above does, and you can switch it back to the ordinary fitted line to compare the two.

What this costs you

This result rests on a few assumptions, and each one marks a boundary.

First, we assumed straight demand and supply lines with additive shocks that are uncorrelated with each other. If the shocks are correlated, for instance because an economic boom raises buyers' incomes and sellers' input costs at the same time, the true slope can fall outside the two-regression bracket entirely.

Second, we looked at a single market in a static equilibrium, with no inventory cycles and no expectations about future prices.

And finally, recovering the demand slope relied on an instrument that shifts supply and is strictly excluded from demand. That assumption has to be defended with what we know about how the market works, because statistical tests alone can't establish it.

Where this leaves the fitted line

It's tempting to think that collecting more transactions will eventually uncover the true demand curve, but this isn't a sample-size problem. A million observations would only estimate the same mix of demand and supply very precisely.

Instead, the demand slopes that fit the data form what's called an identified set, which is every value that could have produced the same population distribution under our assumptions. That's quite different from a confidence interval. As the data grows, sampling error shrinks towards zero, but the identified set stays as wide as the bracket between the forward and reverse regressions.

So when both sides of a market are moving, a line of best fit describes neither what buyers are willing to pay nor what it costs sellers to produce. Its R² tells us how still one curve was compared with the other, and 1/R² is exactly how much ambiguity is left.

What's behind the drift

SURPLUS AND EFFICIENCY

The deadweight loss triangle only counts the trades that don't happen. When a price ceiling rations the goods at random, there's a second loss, and as we'll see, in a linear market the two together come to exactly the size of the cut.

Martin Le, September 2026

When supply meets demand in a competitive market, the quantity traded makes the total surplus as large as it can be. Total surplus adds up, over every unit traded, what the buyer valued it at minus what it cost the seller to make, and a market that maximises it is Pareto efficient, which means nobody can be made better off without making someone else worse off.

When a government restricts output, with a production quota, a tariff or a price ceiling, first courses usually show the harm as a shaded wedge called the deadweight loss triangle, or Harberger triangle, which is the surplus from the trades that no longer happen. The triangle shrinks with the square of the quantity cut, so a small restriction costs very little: a 10% shortfall in supply, for example, costs only 1% of the total surplus. That's why the cost of small interventions has often been treated as minor.

However, the triangle is only half the accounting. When a price ceiling holds quantity down and stops the market from clearing, the units that still trade have to be rationed somehow. If they go out by lottery, by queueing or through personal connections, rather than to whoever values them most, there's a second loss, the misallocation loss. As we'll see, it's first order, which means it shrinks only in proportion to the cut, while the triangle is second order and shrinks with the cut's square.

Where this leaves the triangle

Economists have long debated whether the deadweight losses from market interventions are big enough to worry about, and the triangle on its own suggests they usually aren't. Once we also count who ends up with the goods, the picture changes in four ways.

First, the triangle really is small. Trade stops closest to the margin, where the gains from each trade are thinnest, so the loss from the missing trades grows with the square of the cut. A cut of d costs only d² of the total surplus, so a 10% cut costs just 1% of the gains from trade.

Second, rationing is first order. When a shortage is resolved by queues, lotteries or bureaucracy rather than by prices, the units no longer go to the buyers who value them most. That misallocation loss grows as d(1 − d), and it's bigger than the triangle for every cut under 50%. At a 10% cut, rationing costs nine times as much as the triangle.

Third, the total loss equals the cut itself. In any linear market with random rationing, the two losses add up exactly: d² + d(1 − d) = d. So a 27% shortfall in quantity loses exactly 27% of the total surplus.

And finally, the policy instrument decides the cost. Holding quantity down with a quota costs d², while holding it down with a price ceiling costs d, which is 1/d times as much. At a 10% cut, the price ceiling is ten times as costly as the quota.

Let's start with a competitive market, where supply meets demand. The quantity traded there makes the total surplus as large as it can be. Now let's say a government restricts output, with a production quota, a tariff or a price ceiling. so it grows with the square of the rate, and doubling the rate quadruples the loss. At the peak, every pound the tax raises costs buyers and sellers another fifty pence in lost surplus.

What this costs you

Our market is simple on purpose, and three of its simplifications matter here.

First, we've assumed that buyers and sellers respond to the prices they face, whoever pays the tax. In practice, people respond more to taxes they can see, so where a tax is shown can change behaviour as well as paperwork.

Second, our market is competitive. When a seller has some power over its price, incidence becomes a question of pass-through, how much of a cost change reaches the price, and pass-through can be more than 100%. The article on markup and elasticity looks at that.

And finally, how easily each side can walk away depends on the time horizon. A side that can't respond this year may respond a great deal over a decade, so the short-run incidence of a tax isn't its long-run incidence.

Where this leaves the tax

Who hands over the money doesn't change who pays. In a competitive market, the burden goes to whichever side finds it harder to walk away, and in a straight-line market the buyers' share is S/(B + S) at any rate. In other markets, the familiar elasticity rule is right for small taxes and drifts as they grow.

And at the rate that raises the most money, half the trades are gone and the lost surplus is half the revenue, whatever the slopes. So the top of the revenue curve is an expensive place for a tax to be.

Cost Curves

The textbook envelope picture shows short-run curves touching the long-run curve at their own lowest points. That picture is true at exactly one output — everywhere else, firms build a plant they deliberately run off-peak.

Every introductory microeconomics course presents a famous diagram: a sequence of U-shaped short-run average cost curves nestled against a smooth, sweeping curve underneath them. We are taught that this boundary is the long-run average cost curve — the lower envelope representing the lowest possible unit cost achievable when a firm is free to build any plant size it wishes.

Almost every textbook draws this picture with a subtle, pervasive flaw. It shows each short-run curve touching the long-run curve at that short-run curve's own lowest point. That picture is mathematically impossible everywhere except at a single, unique output.

In 1931, the renowned economist Jacob Viner famously instructed his draughtsman, Dr. Y.K. Wong, to draw a long-run envelope that passed through the minimum point of every short-run curve while never rising above any of them. Wong objected that such a line could not be drawn. Viner insisted, later acknowledging in a sheepish footnote that Wong was right and the geometry was unyielding.

Fixed and Variable Costs

Total cost splits into a fixed component ($F = {fixedCost}$) that must be paid regardless of volume, and a cubic variable component that rises sharply as plant capacity is strained.

Figure 1. The anatomy of short-run cost. Total cost (solid dark line) starts at fixed overhead ({fixedCost}) and curves upward as output rises. At small outputs, fixed overhead dominates; at large outputs, the variable term drives costs upward.

To understand why the envelope behaves as it does, let us examine how a firm's costs are constructed. In the short run, capital equipment and factories cannot be altered instantaneously. A firm operates with a fixed plant size $k$, paying a fixed capital fee $r \cdot k$ alongside an overhead licence fee $f$.

Producing output $q$ requires variable inputs such as labour and raw materials. Because existing machines become congested as volume climbs, marginal productivity declines and variable costs escalate rapidly — here modelled as $w \cdot q^3 / k$. A larger plant dampens this congestion, making high outputs cheaper to produce, but incurs higher fixed overheads.

Average vs Marginal Cost

{#if !showConstantMC} Marginal cost cuts average cost at average cost's exact minimum ($q = {qMinU.toFixed(1)}$). The ratio $MC / AC$ is the output elasticity of cost: when elasticity is below 1, average cost falls; when above 1, it rises. {:else} Without a rising marginal cost, average cost ($100/q + 5$) falls forever across all outputs. The textbook U-shape requires both a fixed cost and rising marginal cost. {/if}

Figure 2. Why marginal cost cuts average cost at its minimum. If producing one extra unit costs less than the current average ($MC < AC$), the new unit drags the average down. If it costs more ($MC > AC$), it pulls the average up. The crossover point must therefore be the exact minimum of average cost.

Dividing total expenditure by output produces average cost ($AC = C/q$). Taking the derivative produces marginal cost ($MC = dC/dq$). Whenever marginal cost lies below average cost, the next unit produced costs less than the running average, pulling average cost downward. When marginal cost rises above average cost, it pulls average cost upward.

It follows as an identity that marginal cost cuts average cost at the exact minimum of the average cost curve. The ratio $MC / AC$ is mathematically identical to the output elasticity of cost: at the minimum of average cost, this elasticity is precisely one.

The Long-Run Lower Envelope

In the long run, firms can adjust capital scale $k$. The long-run average cost curve (thick dark curve) forms the lower envelope of all short-run curves: for any target output $q$, it identifies the minimum possible unit cost by choosing the optimal plant size.

Figure 3. The lower envelope. Every short-run curve sits strictly on or above the thick black long-run curve. But look closely at where each short-run curve touches the envelope: does it touch at its own lowest point?

In the long run, time horizons expand and capital is no longer fixed. If a firm expects to produce a given volume $q$, it can tailor the factory size $k$ to minimise total expenditure. Setting the marginal benefit of capital against its rental cost yields the cost-minimising plant size $k^*(q) = \sqrt{w/r} \cdot q^{1.5}$.

Evaluating short-run cost at this optimal plant size traces the long-run average cost curve ($LRAC$). The long-run curve is literally the lower envelope of all conceivable plant sizes: at each output, it matches the short-run curve of the plant designed for that volume, while sitting strictly below all other plants.

Why Nobody Noticed: The Flat Maximum

If firms run plants off-peak, why does the textbook mistake persist? Because the envelope is locally flat: near the cost minimum, the penalty for building the wrong plant is strictly second-order ($O(e^2)$).

Halving the error quarters the penalty: at $e = 0.001$, the penalty is $0.000043\%$; at $e = 0.002$, it is $0.000174\%$ ($3.996\times$). The envelope's mathematical flatness is what makes the drawing error invisible and the managerial decision remarkably forgiving at the same time.

Why did economists and textbooks continue drawing the tangency at the minimum for generations without anyone complaining? The answer lies in the flatness of the cost envelope.

Because cost functions are smooth and convex, the penalty for building a slightly suboptimal plant size is strictly second-order ($O(e^2)$). As shown in the table above, an error of $\pm 5\%$ in factory capacity increases total production cost by barely $0.1\%$. Even a catastrophic $50\%$ miscalculation raises costs by only $7.3\%$. Model Limits & Boundary Conditions

Every economic model simplifies reality to isolate a mechanism. In this analysis:

Fixed vs variable factor divisibility: Capital is treated as a continuous choice in the long run but completely rigid in the short run. Real factories face adjustment costs, lumpiness, and multi-period construction delays.

Cubic cost structure: Variable costs scale as $q^3$, reflecting rising congestion of fixed machines. While this guarantees clean closed forms, empirical cost curves often exhibit broad flat bottoms before capacity constraints bind.

Absence of market demand: This explainer explores the cost side in isolation. In the subsequent article, Perfect Competition, we introduce market demand and price-taking entry to see how market clearing selects the equilibrium firm scale.

Conclusion

The classic diagram of short-run average cost curves sitting inside a long-run envelope is one of the most familiar pictures in microeconomics. Yet its textbook presentation conceals an exact geometric truth:

The textbook picture holds at exactly one point. When a firm chooses plant size $k$ to minimise the cost of output $q$, the short-run curve is tangent to the long-run envelope at that output. But the plant is run at its own cheapest scale only when $k = f$ — the single plant that serves the global minimum of the long-run curve.

The tangency offset has an exact closed form. The ratio of the tangency output to the plant's cheapest scale is $(2k/(f+k))^{1/3}$. Small plants ($k < f$) touch the envelope on their falling segment, deliberately operating below their efficient scale; large plants ($k > f$) touch on their rising segment, deliberately operating above it.

The firm is running the right plant at the wrong scale. The envelope theorem ensures that marginal costs still align perfectly ($SRMC = LRMC$) at the tangency point. Because the envelope is locally flat, the cost penalty for operating off-peak is strictly second-order ($O(e^2)$) — explaining why Jacob Viner's draughtsman could not draw what his professor requested, and why the error remained innocuous for so long.

Perfect Competition

Textbooks teach that free entry drives price down to minimum average cost and wipes out all economic profit. But firms are whole numbers: entry stops early, profit never vanishes, and the long-run supply curve is a sawtooth.

In standard economic textbooks, perfect competition is introduced as an idealised state: thousands of atomistic price-takers, price equal to marginal cost, and long-run economic profits ground down to absolute zero.

Yet in real markets, firms are not infinitesimal dust. They are indivisible legal and physical entities that enter one at a time. Each entrant brings its own overhead and adds a discrete chunk of output to market supply.

When firms are whole numbers, the classical picture of a flat, frictionless horizontal supply curve fractures. In its place emerges a dynamic sequence of entry decisions, persistent integer rents, and a long-run supply curve that is not flat at all, but a sawtooth.

THE WORKED RUN: WHY ENTRY HALTS AT 50

In our baseline market (A = 600, B = 10), continuous zero-profit arithmetic would require n̄ = 50.71 firms. But factories cannot arrive in decimal fractions.

Minimum average cost is min AC = 24.1421. The 50th firm leaves the market price at $24.2857 — exactly $0.1436 above minimum average cost. The 51st firm would push the price below $24.1421, inflicting a private loss of $0.4067. Because entry is voluntary, firm 51 refuses to enter. The incumbent 50 firms retain their profit perpetually.

Now consider the critical friction: firms cannot enter in fractions.

If market demand requires $n^* = 59.5$ firms to drive price exactly to $AC^* = 20$, what happens? The 59th firm enters happily, earning positive profits. But a potential 60th entrant calculates post-entry price: if it enters, the market will be flooded, price will drop to $\$19.93$, and all 60 firms will lose $\$0.66$ per period.

The 60th firm stays out. Free entry halts at $n = 59$. As a result, the 59 incumbent firms enjoy a persistent integer rent ($\pi = \$4.35$ each) that cannot be competed away by free entry. The lumpiness of the firm acts as a natural barrier to entry.

At smaller market scales, the discrete teeth of the long-run supply curve are clearly visible. Price drops to minimum average cost ($24.14$) only at the exact knife-edge points where market size accommodates an exact whole number of firms.

For any fixed number of firms $n$, small increases in demand travel up the short-run supply curve, driving price above $20$. Price continues climbing until demand is strong enough to allow an additional firm to break even post-entry. At that tipping point, an entrant arrives, market supply jumps discretely, and price snaps back down toward minimum average cost.

Industrial economists often warn of a "business-stealing externality" where too many firms enter an industry. Under competitive price taking, however, free entry is never excessive. Over 30,000 random markets, the market firm count n* is either exactly optimal (50.4%) or at most one firm short (49.6%).

MODEL ASSUMPTIONS & REAL-WORLD BOUNDARIES

The classical model of perfect competition rests on strict assumptions that provide clean benchmarks:

Homogeneous products: Consumers view goods from all sellers as perfect substitutes. In practice, brand, location, and packaging differentiate products.

Symmetric technology: Every entrant has access to the identical cost curve $C(q)$. In reality, incumbents often enjoy lower costs due to learning-by-doing or prime real estate.

Instantaneous adjustment: Entry occurs without friction as soon as $\pi > 0$. In real industries, permitting, construction, and financing introduce significant lag.

CONCLUSION

The standard competitive story states that free entry drives economic profits completely to zero, forcing market price down to the absolute minimum of average cost. This elegant benchmark is approximately true at scale, but exactly false in reality:

Firms enter as integers, not fractions. The market can only sustain a whole number of firms. Because entry is an all-or-nothing private gamble, entry ceases at n* = ⌊n̄⌋ — the last whole firm that can cover its fixed overhead.

Surviving profit never vanishes. Every firm inside the market earns a positive residual profit equal to ((1 + x)2 − 1) fixed costs, where x = frac(n̄) / (Bd + n*). In a market of 50 firms, this profit is over 2% of a firm's fixed investment, and no prospective entrant can afford to enter to compete it away.

The long-run supply curve is a sawtooth. Across market sizes, the long-run price line repeatedly dips down to minimum average cost at discrete integer thresholds, rising above it in between. While the sawtooth flattens like 1/n as the market expands, free entry under price taking remains strictly non-excessive — always within one firm of the social optimum.

PERFECT COMPETITION

Free entry is meant to push the price down to the lowest average cost. As we'll see, firms come in whole numbers, so entry stops a little short, and the long-run supply curve turns out to be a sawtooth.

Martin Le, September 2026

Let's picture a market with lots of small firms that all sell the same thing, like farms growing the same wheat. None of them is big enough to move the price, so each one takes the market price as given. This is perfect competition, and first courses tell a clean story about how it settles down in the long run.

While the firms in the market make a profit, firms outside it have a reason to come in. Each newcomer adds supply, which pushes the price down, and entry carries on until the profit is gone. By then the price has fallen to the lowest average cost a firm can reach, so the long-run supply curve is flat at that level.

What this costs you

Our market is simple on purpose, and three of its assumptions matter here.

First, every firm has the same costs. In real markets, firms that got in early or found better sites often have lower costs, and they can keep a profit that has nothing to do with whole numbers.

Second, entry is instant and free. Real entrants need permits, buildings and money, which take time and can hold entry back even when there's a profit to be had.

And finally, every firm sells exactly the same product. Once products differ, firms get some power over their prices, and the story changes. That's the subject of the article on monopolistic competition.

Where this leaves free entry

Free entry really does pull the price down towards the lowest average cost, and in a big market it gets very close. What it can't do, except by luck, is get there exactly, because the market has to hold a whole number of firms. The last firm in makes a small profit, the next one would make a loss, and so entry stops a little early.

That leaves us with a long-run supply curve shaped like a saw, whose teeth shrink as the market grows. It also leaves the market at most one firm short of the number a planner would choose, and never over it, which is a reassuring thing to know about perfect competition.

MARKUP AND ELASTICITY

The most-quoted rule in pricing is markup = 1/|elasticity|, and it is exactly true — at every profit-maximising price, on every demand curve. It is also a statement about a single point. Two demand curves through that point with the same elasticity set the same price to the last digit, and one of them passes half a cost increase through while the other passes two and a half times.

Martin Le, September 2026

Let's put ourselves in the pricing seat. You sell one product, you know your marginal cost, and you've run an experiment: at the price you're charging, demand has an elasticity of 5/3 — raise the price 3% and quantity falls about 5%. The most-quoted rule in pricing says this is all you need to know. It's called the Lerner rule, and it says your profit-maximising markup over cost is exactly one over that elasticity:

𝑝 − 𝑐 𝑝 = 1 ∣ 𝜀 ∣ p p−c ​ = ∣ε∣ 1 ​

The rule is not approximately true. It is exactly true, at the optimum, on every demand curve there is — because it is the first-order condition in disguise, and we'll write that out. What the rule is not is a passport to the rest of the demand curve. Let's find out how much it doesn't tell you, with two curves that agree on everything the rule can see.

Why the rule is true everywhere at once

Maximise profit, which is price minus cost, times quantity. The first-order condition says: at the best price, a small rise in price gains margin on the units you keep and loses exactly the units you lose:

( 𝑝 − 𝑐 ) 𝑞 ′ ( 𝑝 ) + 𝑞 ( 𝑝 ) = 0 (p−c)q ′ (p)+q(p)=0

Divide through by q and rearrange, and the left-hand side is the Lerner ratio and the right-hand side is 1/|ε| — where ε is the elasticity at the price you settled on. That last clause is doing more work than it looks like. The rule doesn't predict where you'll price; it tells you what the elasticity will be once you get there. You could say the elasticity doesn't determine the markup so much as the markup goes looking for the point where the elasticity suits it.

Two curves, one point

So let's build the test. We'll take a straight-line demand curve and a constant-elasticity demand curve, and pin them together: both pass through the price 25 and quantity 45, and both have elasticity exactly 5/3 there. At your measured point they are indistinguishable. The straight line is 120 − 3p; the constant-elasticity curve is 45 × (p/25)^(−5/3). Set marginal cost to 10 and the rule reads 1/(5/3) = 60% off both — so both curves' optimal price should be 25. And it is, to the last decimal, for both.

At c = 10 both curves put the price at exactly 25.0 and the markup at 60% — 1/|ε|, as the rule says. You cannot tell them apart from here.

Now nudge the cost

Everything you'd use the elasticity for is a counterfactual: input prices move, and you want to know what happens to your price. Drag the cost off 10 and the two curves — identical at the pin — come apart immediately. The straight line passes through exactly half of any cost increase, always, whoever you are. The constant-elasticity curve passes through more than all of it: at ε = 5/3 a one-unit cost rise lifts the optimal price by 2.5 units, at ε = 2 it lifts by 2, and the general rule is ε/(ε−1). Same point, same elasticity, same price today — and a cost shock tomorrow that is five times the size.

Lerner = 1/|ε| on every row, for both curves (worst gap 1.0e-12) — and the prices still split at every cost but one.

One family, one slider

There's a single family of demand curves that contains both of ours — a power of an affine function — and one exponent, n, sweeps you from the straight line (n = 1) through ever-flatter constant-elasticity curves (n negative) and past a genuinely strange pocket in between. Every member is pinned through the same point with the same elasticity, so the lab below can hold your measured data fixed and move only the thing the elasticity cannot see: the curvature.

𝑞 ( 𝑝 ) = ( 𝑎 + 𝑏 𝑝 ) 𝑛 q(p)=(a+bp) n

Drag n. The optimum stays at 25, the markup stays at 60%, and 1/|ε| at the optimum stays equal to it to four decimals — the rule never budges. What budges is the answer to every question you actually hired the elasticity to answer.

n = 1.00: the optimum is still p = 25.00, the markup is still 60%, and 1/ε there is 1.6667 — but a one-unit cost rise now shifts the price by 0.50 units.

The pass-through law

Differentiate the first-order condition with respect to cost and the whole story falls out in one line:

𝜌 = 𝑑 𝑝 ∗ 𝑑 𝑐 = 𝑞 ′ 2 𝑞 ′ + ( 𝑝 − 𝑐 ) 𝑞 ′ ′ ρ= dc dp ∗ ​ = 2q ′ +(p−c)q ′′ q ′ ​

Pass-through reads the second derivative — the curvature — and the elasticity contains none of it. For the whole family the answer collapses to ρ = n/(1 + n): exactly a half on the straight line, ε/(ε−1) down the constant-elasticity branch, one in the perfect-competition limit, and — in the pocket between n = −1 and n = 0 — negative, where a cost rise lowers the optimal price. That perverse case is real in the literature, not a curiosity of the algebra; it is one of the things curvature has been hiding from the point estimate.

The half-full stop at n = 1 is the straight line; everything left of the asymptote over-shifts; the pocket between −1 and 0 is the perverse case where a cost rise lowers the optimal price.

What this costs you

First, an elasticity is a local estimate, and everything above says exactly how far local gets you: the price today, and nothing about any response. If a decision depends on pass-through — a tax argument, a tariff, an input shock — the curvature has to be estimated or assumed, and the assumption is doing the work.

Second, the markup rule is an identity at the optimum, so observing it hold is no evidence that you are optimising; a firm charging anywhere can compute 1/|ε| at its own price and get some number. What the rule gives you is a consistency check and a direction, not a destination.

And third, the elasticity that belongs in the formula is the elasticity of your demand — the residual demand after rivals respond — which is usually far more elastic than the market elasticity a report hands you. Practitioners lose more money to that substitution than to curvature.

In one sentence

The markup rule is exactly true and exactly silent: it tells you where you are, and curvature — the thing it cannot see — decides where you go. Thanks for reading!

Monopolistic Competition

Why product variety is not free, why real firms face less price sensitivity than textbook models claim, and the exact arithmetic of the scale-variety tradeoff.

In pure perfect competition, all sellers provide identical commodities. In pure monopoly, a single firm supplies the entire industry. Yet the overwhelming majority of modern consumer markets—coffee shops, restaurants, software apps, craft breweries, and sneakers—inhabit the rich middle ground of monopolistic competition.

Each business produces a distinctive variety that consumers cherish. If your favourite café raises prices by ten cents, you do not instantly abandon them for a competitor down the road. But neither can the café charge fifty dollars a cup: close substitutes surround it.

Operating with unexhausted scale economies is not a market failure; it is the necessary cost of enjoying differentiated goods.

Model Limits & Boundary Conditions

The Dixit-Stiglitz model provides an exceptionally tractable foundation, with specific simplifications:

Symmetric substitution: Every variety substitutes equally well for every other variety. In physical retail or localized services, spatial competition (Hotelling / Salop circle) introduces localized rivalry.

Constant expenditure share: Total sector spending $E$ is invariant to price. In general equilibrium, consumer spending on the differentiated sector responds to prices in other sectors.

Identical cost structures: Entrants all pay identical overhead $f$ and marginal cost $c$. In heterogeneous firm models (e.g. Melitz 2003), productivity varies widely across entrants.

Conclusion

Monopolistic competition bridges the wide chasm between pure price-taking competition and isolated single-firm monopoly. By integrating product differentiation and free entry, the Dixit-Stiglitz framework reveals four precise principles:

Elasticity depends on market share. When an industry contains n varieties, a single firm's true price elasticity is σ − (σ − 1)/n. In concentrated markets, firms recognize their weight in the aggregate price index, softening price competition.

The scale shortfall is exactly 1/n. Each firm produces 1 − 1/n of the asymptotic large-group output. As market size expands, individual firms expand up to their capacity bound f(σ − 1)/c.

Market variety is remarkably close to optimal. The free market creates at most (σ − 1)/σ excess varieties compared to a social planner. Because (σ − 1)/σ < 1, decentralized entry overprovides by less than a single whole firm!

"Excess capacity" is not wasted efficiency. Real industries face overhead costs and relatively constant marginal costs. Average cost slopes downward everywhere. The sacrifice in firm scale is the price society willingly pays for the richness of consumer choice.

MONOPOLISTIC COMPETITION

When every firm sells a slightly different product, how many varieties does a market end up with? As we'll see, a finite number of firms adds a small correction to each familiar answer, and every correction fades as one over the number of firms.

Martin Le, September 2026

Let's think about the cafés on a busy street. Each one sells coffee, but not quite the same coffee, because the beans, the seats and the walk from your door all differ. So if one café raises its price a little, it loses some of its customers but not all of them. That's a small amount of power over the price, which a perfectly competitive firm doesn't have. On the other hand, there are plenty of other cafés, so none of them can charge whatever it likes.

This mix is called monopolistic competition: each firm has a monopoly on its own variety, and it competes with everyone else's. It raises a question that neither perfect competition nor monopoly asks. Every new café costs a fixed amount to open, so more variety means more fixed costs and less coffee per café. How many varieties does a market end up with, and is that the right number?

The standard way to answer this is a model that Avinash Dixit and Joseph Stiglitz published in 1977. We usually meet its "large-group" version, in which each firm is too small to matter to the market as a whole. As we'll see, if we keep the number of firms finite, a few of the familiar results pick up a small correction, and each correction fades as one over the number of firms.

What this costs you

The model is simple on purpose, and three of its simplifications matter here.

First, every variety substitutes for every other one equally well. Real cafés compete mostly with their neighbours, and models of competition along a street or around a circle are built to capture that.

Second, total spending in the market stays fixed whatever the prices are, and every firm has the same costs. In models where productivity differs between firms, such as Melitz (2003), the better firms grow and the worse ones leave, and that changes the story.

And finally, we've let n take any value, as if firms could come in fractions. The article on perfect competition shows what whole numbers do to entry.

Where this leaves monopolistic competition

Dixit and Stiglitz's model gives us a clean account of a market full of varieties. Each firm has a little power over its price, entry keeps profit at zero, and the market ends up with slightly more varieties than a planner would choose.

Keeping the number of firms finite adds three corrections that the large-group version leaves out. The elasticity each firm faces is σ − (σ − 1)/n rather than σ, each firm makes 1/n less than the large-group scale, and the extra varieties never add up to a whole firm. All three fade as the market grows, which is why the large-group results are a good guide to big markets and a slightly rosy one to small markets.
