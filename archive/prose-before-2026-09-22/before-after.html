ECONOMIC RENT
Seven technologies can do one job, and a rectangle test — the one every first course teaches — removes two of them and crowns a sensible-looking middle option. That option survives every test there is, and it is the cheapest way to do the job at no price at all. Rent is the reward for being temporarily different, and competition is what destroys it and what spreads the innovation.
Martin Le, September 2026
Let's suppose you run one batch job a month, and it has to be delivered. There is more than one way to do it. You can throw hardware at the thing and keep the engineers away from it, or you can have people hand-tune it and rent almost no machines at all, and there are sensible-looking options in between. Each way is a recipe: so many engineer-days of someone's time, so many machine-days of rented compute, in fixed proportions. Twice the job means twice both, which is what makes the whole thing drawable.
Most first courses in economics have a lesson about choosing between inputs like these, and it comes with a test you can run yourself. It's worth running that test on our seven recipes before we start poking at it, because the test is genuinely useful and it is also, as we'll see, the reason a perfectly good technology can sit there chosen by nobody.
Legacy needs 9 engineer-days and 30 machine-days, and Batched and Indexed need less of both, so the rectangle test removes legacy.
What a choice depends on
Say an engineer-day costs w and a machine-day costs p. A recipe that needs N of the first and R of the second then costs $w\,N + p\,R$ a run, and the whole of that cost carries one number that matters:
$c = w\,N + p\,R = p\,(r\,N + R)$
Everything scales by p, so which recipe is cheapest depends on r, the relative price of the two inputs, and on nothing else. Double the wages and double the machine prices and every cost doubles with them, so the ranking cannot move. That's a useful thing to know before you argue about prices: only the ratio between them can change anybody's mind.
Here's the picture that follows. Fix a recipe and let r move, and its cost is a straight line with slope N and intercept R. So each recipe is a point in input space and a line in cost space, and the cheapest available cost is the lower envelope of those lines. A recipe is ever chosen exactly when its line touches that envelope.
At r = 2.00, Indexed runs the job for 26 machine-days, and it is the cheapest technology there is.

The money never matters on its own: at 600 an engineer-day and 200 a machine-day the relative price is r = 3, and doubling both leaves the choice alone, because every cost carries the same factor.
The envelope has three kinks, and they sit at relative prices of exactly 1, 3 and 8. People cheap means Brute force; as engineers get dearer the industry moves to Batched, then Indexed, then Hand-tuned, and the slope of the envelope between the kinks is always the first input of the recipe in charge — which is the industry's demand for engineers, read straight off a cost curve.
The one that never gets picked
Now for the trap. There's a seventh recipe we kept off the first chart. It's called Balanced, it needs 5 engineer-days and 20 machine-days a run, and it sits politely in the middle of every trade-off on the list. Run the rectangle test on it and nothing beats it. Ask any of the other six to fight it and Balanced wins the argument: it uses less of at least one input than whatever attacked it.
And it is the cheapest way to run the job at no price at all. Not at a silly price — at no price, anywhere between engineers nearly free and machines nearly free. Every course teaches the rectangle test, and this is what it cannot see: surviving the test means only that nothing dominates you, not that anybody wants you.
At 20 machine-days, BALANCED is the cheapest technology at no relative price at all. Beaten by nothing, chosen never.
The window closed at 18 machine-days, exactly where the chord between BATCHED and INDEXED sits at five engineer-days. At 20 machine-days it is the cheapest choice nowhere.
There is a clean reason Balanced never wins. Between Batched and Indexed the industry's cost is a V with its point at r = 3, and a recipe can only join that envelope by touching it from below. Balanced's line passes above the chord joining its two neighbours, so it never does. Give Balanced fewer machine-days and a window opens; from 14 machine-days up the window is exactly 18 minus its own machine-days wide, and it slams shut at 18, which is the chord's height at five engineer-days. The arithmetic doesn't care that Balanced looks reasonable. Reasonable isn't a price.
What the pioneer earns
Suppose the industry has settled at r = 2, the standard regime. The job runs on Indexed for 26 machine-day-equivalents, and Legacy — the way things were done before — would cost 48. Whoever holds Indexed can sell the run for anything up to 48 and still beat the alternative, so there is a gap of 22 sitting under the price. That gap is economic rent: what a thing earns because it is temporarily better than the next best way, and not because of what it costs.
$\text{rent}(t, r) = (r\,N_t + R_t) - \min_{t'}(r\,N_{t'} + R_{t'})$
Drag the relative price below and hold different technologies. The rent is zero exactly when the technology you're holding is the frontier — which is another way of saying that the frontier is where the rent has already been competed away.
At r = 2.00 the job runs for 26 on Indexed, and holding Hand-tuned costs 34. The gap is 8.0 machine-days a run, about 1.6k at 200 a machine-day, and that gap is the rent.
Why competition both kills the rent and spreads the idea
Rent is a target. A rival who adopts Indexed can copy the pioneer's product, offer the same run a little cheaper, and keep the difference for as long as the undercut stays under the gap. The same arithmetic is what spreads the innovation. The gain to switching from Legacy to Indexed is 22 machine-days at r = 2, and it grows by exactly 2 for every unit the relative price rises, because the switch sheds 2 engineer-days. When engineers get dearer, adopting the labour-lean technology gets worth faster — so the arrival that destroyed one rent is carried round the industry by the rents it creates as prices move. Being temporarily different is profitable, and the competition that ends the profit is also the mechanism that copies the difference.

What this diagram costs you
First, this is a partial-equilibrium story: one job, one month, two inputs, no capacity constraints and no sunk costs. The rent here is a technology gap; real rents also come from patents, licences, brand and network effects, and those live longer than an undercut cycle.
Second, we've measured the rent in machine-day-equivalents, which is honest but a little abstract; turning it into money needs a price for a run, and that price is doing the rent's own work in this model.
And third, competition here is a price war between recipes. The process that actually diffs an innovation through an industry — imitation with a lag, licensing, staff turnover — is behind the envelope's movement, not on top of it.
In one sentence
The rectangle test tells you what nobody dominates; the envelope tells you what everybody wants; the gap between the two is where rent lives, and competition closes it from below while it copies the reason it opened. Thanks for reading!

MICROECONOMICS · THE IDENTIFICATION PROBLEM
SUPPLY & DEMAND
Why market data never draws the demand curve — and how its goodness of fit is the identification failure, read backwards.
Every introductory economics textbook begins with the same iconic diagram: a downward-sloping demand curve representing consumer willingness to pay, and an upward-sloping supply curve representing producer costs. Where the two scissors cross, the market finds its clearing price and quantity.
This picture is clean, intuitive, and mathematically simple. But when a quantitative practitioner actually enters a market to measure real transactions, those neat lines are nowhere to be found. Instead, we observe a noisy scatter of prices and quantities recorded month by month. The standard impulse is to fit a line through that cloud with ordinary least squares and declare that we have found the demand curve.
That impulse is almost always wrong. In fact, a line fitted through market transactions is neither demand nor supply: it is a weighted mixture of both, and the statistical goodness of fit everyone is taught to prize is the very factor measuring our failure to pin down the truth.
THE ONE CROSSING
Before we can interpret a whole cloud of market transactions, let's watch one equilibrium move. When only demand shifts, the crossing slides along the fixed supply curve. When only supply shifts, the crossing slides along the fixed demand curve.
Current equilibrium: price p = 20.0, quantity q = 60.0. Both curves are at their base position (p₀ = 20, q₀ = 60).
To understand why a cloud of transactions can be so deceptive, consider what causes the market crossing to move in the first place. When unobserved factors — such as consumer tastes or household incomes — shift the demand curve, suppliers respond by moving along their cost structure. As demand moves back and forth, the resulting equilibria trace out the supply curve.
Conversely, when input costs or weather conditions shift supply, consumers adjust their purchases along their valuation schedule, tracing out the demand curve. In other words, a curve is revealed only when it stands perfectly still while the other curve does all the moving.
Now let's test what happens when both forces operate simultaneously.

In truth, demand in this market is entirely orthodox: consumers buy substantially less when the price rises (slope = −3.0). The positive slope appeared because demand shocks were larger and more frequent than supply shocks over those eighteen months. The data was reflecting supply responses to shifting demand, not consumer demand itself.

THE MECHANISM: A STRAIGHT LINE IN SHOCK SHARE
Why does the cloud rotate? Because the ordinary least squares slope is a simple convex combination of the two slopes: slope = w · S − (1 − w) · B. Move your cursor across the chart to trace the fitted slope across all possible shock shares.
At w = 0.35, the fitted slope is -1.25. The fitted slope divides the interval [−3, +2] in exactly the ratio of the shock variances: τd²/τs² = 0.54.
As the shock mix shifts from w = 0 to w = 1, the ordinary least squares slope changes according to the linear equation slope = w · S − (1 − w) · B. Equivalently, the fitted slope divides the interval [−B, S] in the exact ratio of the two shock variances, $\tau_d^2 / \tau_s^2$.
This produces a dangerous pitfall at w* = B / (B + S) = 0.60. At this exact ratio, the covariance between price and quantity is zero, and the cloud is completely horizontal. A casual glance might suggest that demand is perfectly inelastic or that consumers are indifferent to price. In reality, demand is highly price-sensitive, but its downward slope is perfectly cancelled in the covariance by upward-sloping supply shocks.
THE R² PARADOX: HIGH FIT MEANS ZERO IDENTIFICATION
Econometricians and data scientists are taught to trust regressions with a high R². But in market data, R² = 1 at both extremes: when you are estimating true demand (w = 0) and when you are estimating true supply (w = 1). A high R² tells you that one curve was still; it cannot tell you which one.
At w = 0.35, R² = 0.216. As R² decreases, the data leaves the true demand slope free over an expanding interval.
This leads directly to the identification problem, first articulated by Philip and Elmer Working in 1927. Standard statistical metrics cannot rescue us here. In fact, $R^2 = 1.0$ at both ends of the spectrum: when the cloud fits demand perfectly, and when it fits supply perfectly. At the flat cloud ($w^* = 0.60$), $R^2 = 0$.
A high $R^2$ simply tells us that one curve was substantially more stable than the other; it provides zero evidence as to which curve stood still.
THE BRACKET IDENTITY: GOODNESS OF FIT IS IDENTIFICATION FAILURE
When market transactions slope downward, two empirical bounds contain the true demand curve. The forward regression (fitting quantity on price) gives a lower bound B_lo. The reverse regression (fitting price on quantity and solving backwards) gives an upper bound B_hi. The ratio between them is not an approximation: B_hi / B_lo = 1 / R² exactly.
Solid green line: forward regression (quantity on price). Dashed green line: reverse regression (price on quantity). Blue line: true demand. As R² falls, the two regression lines scissor apart, widening the identification bracket by a factor of exactly 1/R².
If we cannot identify a single demand curve, what can we say about the market? In 1984, Steven Klepper and Edward Leamer proved that when transaction data slopes downward, the observed moments still establish strict diagnostic bounds.
The standard forward regression of quantity on price yields a conservative lower bound for the demand slope magnitude, B_lo = −Cov(p, q) / Var(p). The reverse regression of price on quantity, inverted, yields an upper bound, B_hi = −Var(q) / Cov(p, q).

THE SOLUTION: AN EXOGENOUS SHIFTER
More observations cannot solve the identification problem: millions of data points merely estimate the confounded mixture with greater precision. What breaks the deadlock is an instrument — a variable that shifts one curve without entering the other.

How do economists break out of this bracket? The answer is not more observations: increasing the sample size simply estimates the confounded moments with greater precision, leaving the $1/R^2$ bracket unchanged.
Instead, we require an instrumental variable: an external shock $Z$ that shifts supply without entering consumer demand. By measuring how average price and quantity change across different states of the instrument, the two-point slope isolates pure supply shifts and recovers the true demand curve.
THE IDENTIFIED SET AND ITS LIMITS
When we look at market data, it is tempting to believe that collecting more transactions will eventually uncover the true demand curve. But this is not a sample-size problem. A million observations will merely estimate the confounded mixture of demand and supply with exquisite precision.
Instead, the range of demand curves compatible with observed market moments forms an identified set, which is the set of parameter values that could have generated the population distribution under our assumptions. This set is fundamentally different from a confidence interval: sampling error shrinks towards zero as data grows, but the identification bracket remains wide, bounded strictly by the forward regression and the reverse regression.
This result rests on several explicit assumptions that define its boundaries. First, we assumed linear demand and supply curves with additive, uncorrelated shocks. If demand and supply shocks are correlated — for instance, if macroeconomic expansions boost consumer purchasing power while simultaneously inflating input costs — the true slope can fall outside the two-regression bracket entirely. Second, we examined a single market in static equilibrium without inventory cycles or forward-looking price expectations. Finally, our recovery of the demand slope relied on an instrument that shifts supply while being strictly excluded from demand, an assumption that must be justified on institutional grounds rather than statistical tests alone.
Understanding market equilibrium requires recognising what transaction data can and cannot say on its own. When both sides of the market are in motion, a line of best fit describes neither buyer valuation nor seller cost. Goodness of fit measures how still one curve was relative to the other, and its reciprocal is the exact factor of ambiguity that remains.

A perfect fit at both ends

What R² can't tell us
This is the identification problem, which Elmer Working set out in 1927: the data alone can't tell us which curve we're looking at. The usual statistics can't rescue us either, because R² = 1.0 at both ends of the range, when the cloud fits demand perfectly and when it fits supply perfectly. At the flat cloud, where w* = 0.60, R² = 0.
So a high R² only tells us that one curve was much more stable than the other. It gives us no evidence at all about which curve stood still.

A bracket around the demand slope
If we can't pin down the demand curve, what can the data tell us? Edward Leamer showed in 1981 that when the cloud slopes downwards and the two kinds of shock are uncorrelated, the data still puts firm bounds on the demand slope.

An outside shifter recovers demand
More observations can't solve the identification problem, because millions of data points only estimate the same mixture more precisely. What breaks the deadlock is an instrument, which is a variable that shifts one curve without entering the other.
The instrumental-variable slope is −2.84, against a true demand slope of −3.0. Because it uses only the variation that the supply shifter Z causes, the line between the two group averages strips out the demand shocks and recovers the demand curve, apart from sampling noise.
How to break out of the bracket
So how do economists get out of this bracket? More data won't do it. A bigger sample only estimates the same mixed-up moments more precisely, and the 1/R² bracket stays exactly as wide.
What we need instead is an instrumental variable, which is an outside shock Z that shifts supply without affecting demand. If we compare the average price and quantity when the instrument is high with when it's low, the slope between those two points comes only from supply moving, so it traces out the demand curve. That's what the figure above does, and you can switch it back to the ordinary fitted line to compare the two.
What this costs you
This result rests on a few assumptions, and each one marks a boundary.
First, we assumed straight demand and supply lines with additive shocks that are uncorrelated with each other. If the shocks are correlated, for instance because an economic boom raises buyers' incomes and sellers' input costs at the same time, the true slope can fall outside the two-regression bracket entirely.
Second, we looked at a single market in a static equilibrium, with no inventory cycles and no expectations about future prices.
And finally, recovering the demand slope relied on an instrument that shifts supply and is strictly excluded from demand. That assumption has to be defended with what we know about how the market works, because statistical tests alone can't establish it.
Where this leaves the fitted line
It's tempting to think that collecting more transactions will eventually uncover the true demand curve, but this isn't a sample-size problem. A million observations would only estimate the same mix of demand and supply very precisely.
Instead, the demand slopes that fit the data form what's called an identified set, which is every value that could have produced the same population distribution under our assumptions. That's quite different from a confidence interval. As the data grows, sampling error shrinks towards zero, but the identified set stays as wide as the bracket between the forward and reverse regressions.
So when both sides of a market are moving, a line of best fit describes neither what buyers are willing to pay nor what it costs sellers to produce. Its R² tells us how still one curve was compared with the other, and 1/R² is exactly how much ambiguity is left.

ELASTICITY
Why elasticity is not a property of a good, how every demand curve cuts its own tangent into an exact ratio of two lengths, and what two-point formulas secretly assume.
Every economics curriculum introduces price elasticity of demand with a familiar formula: the percentage change in quantity demanded divided by the percentage change in price. We are told that some goods — like petrol, insulin, or basic foodstuffs — are inelastic, while luxury holidays and restaurant meals are elastic.
That framing is fundamentally misleading. Elasticity is not a property of a good at all. On a single, straight demand curve, elasticity takes every value from zero to infinity. The very same good is deeply inelastic at low prices and aggressively elastic at high prices.
What the elasticity number actually is, is a ratio of two geometric lengths. Whenever you pick any point on any demand curve ever drawn, that point cuts its own tangent line into two pieces. The price elasticity of demand is identically the upper segment divided by the lower segment.
Figure 1. The ruler identity. On every demand curve, the tangent at point (p, q) meets the quantity axis at (0, q − p · q') and the price axis at (p − q/q', 0). The upper segment (blue) divided by the lower segment (red) is identically equal to the point elasticity |ε| = |q' · p / q|. When |ε| = 1, the tangent is cut exactly in half.

SURPLUS & EFFICIENCY
Why the textbook triangle is only the second-order half of the accounting — and why the total loss from a price ceiling is exactly the cut itself: d² + d(1 − d) = d.
In microeconomic theory, the market clearing quantity where supply equals demand is praised for achieving Pareto efficiency: it maximises the total surplus, or the sum of consumer valuations minus producer costs across all traded units.
When governments intervene to restrict output — whether through production quotas, tariffs, or price ceilings — introductory courses demonstrate the resulting harm using a familiar shaded wedge: the Harberger deadweight loss triangle. Because this triangle shrinks with the square of the quantity cut, economists have often concluded that the social cost of minor market interference is trivially small. A 10% supply shortfall, after all, sacrifices only 1% of the total surplus.
That reassuring calculation is only half the accounting. When quantity is suppressed by a price ceiling that prevents the market from clearing, the units that still trade must be rationed somehow. If they are distributed by lottery, queueing, or personal ties rather than to whoever values them most, the resulting misallocation loss is not second order: it is first order.
1. The Wrong Quantity
Trade stops at q = 44.0. The unconsumed gains from trade form the classic second-order deadweight loss triangle of size ½k(q* − q)².
2. The Wrong People
At ceiling price p̄ = 12.0, 84 buyers compete for 44.0 units. Random rationing hands units to low-value buyers, costing q(N − q)/(2B).

SCOPE AND ANALYTICAL BOUNDARIES
Our derivations assume linear demand and supply with zero income effects, allowing consumer surplus to serve as an exact monetary metric of welfare (we formalise equivalent and compensating variation in our companion essay, Welfare Measures). If secondary markets operate without transaction costs, resale re-establishes efficient allocation and eliminates misallocation entirely — though physical queueing often dissipates the surplus as waiting costs instead.
Note that the triangle formula $\frac{1}{2} k (q^* - q)^2$ is mathematically identical to the tax deadweight loss $\frac{1}{2} t^2 \frac{BS}{B + S}$ examined in Tax Incidence, where a tax rate $t$ induces a quantity cut $q^* - q = t \frac{BS}{B + S}$. Finally, while this chapter examines the total size of the surplus, the equitable division of that surplus between buyers and sellers is the subject of Bargaining & the Surplus.
Conclusion
Economists have long debated whether deadweight losses from market interventions are large enough to worry about. By treating supply disruptions solely through the lens of Harberger triangles, standard textbooks have systematically underestimated the stakes:
The textbook triangle is genuinely small. Because trade halts closest to the margin where private gains are thinnest, deadweight loss from missing transactions scales quadratically: a cut of fraction d costs only d² of total surplus. A 10% cut costs just 1% of the gains from trade.
Rationing is first order. When shortages are resolved by queues, lotteries, or bureaucratic rationing rather than prices, units no longer flow to the consumers who value them most. That misallocation loss scales as d(1 − d) and dominates the triangle for every cut under 50%. At a 10% cut, rationing costs nine times the triangle.
The total loss equals the cut itself. In any linear market with random rationing, the two losses sum to an exact identity: d² + d(1 − d) = d. A 27% quantity shortfall destroys exactly 27% of the total potential surplus.
The policy instrument decides the cost. Holding quantity down by quota costs d²; holding it down by price ceiling costs d. The ceiling multiplies total economic destruction by exactly 1/d. At a 10% cut, the price ceiling is ten times more expensive than the quota.

When supply meets demand in a competitive market, the quantity traded makes the total surplus as large as it can be. Total surplus adds up, over every unit traded, what the buyer valued it at minus what it cost the seller to make, and a market that maximises it is Pareto efficient, which means nobody can be made better off without making someone else worse off.
When a government restricts output, with a production quota, a tariff or a price ceiling, first courses usually show the harm as a shaded wedge called the deadweight loss triangle, or Harberger triangle, which is the surplus from the trades that no longer happen. The triangle shrinks with the square of the quantity cut, so a small restriction costs very little: a 10% shortfall in supply, for example, costs only 1% of the total surplus. That's why the cost of small interventions has often been treated as minor.
However, the triangle is only half the accounting. When a price ceiling holds quantity down and stops the market from clearing, the units that still trade have to be rationed somehow. If they go out by lottery, by queueing or through personal connections, rather than to whoever values them most, there's a second loss, the misallocation loss. As we'll see, it's first order, which means it shrinks only in proportion to the cut, while the triangle is second order and shrinks with the cut's square.

What this costs you
Like any model, this one leaves things out, and three of them matter here.
First, we assumed straight demand and supply lines and no income effects, which is what lets consumer surplus serve as an exact measure of welfare in money. With curved demand the losses change, and the figure above shows that in one such case, a constant-elasticity demand curve, rationing costs even more.
Second, we assumed that buyers can't resell. If they could resell at no cost, resale would move the units to the buyers who value them most and remove the misallocation loss entirely. On the other hand, queueing often uses up the surplus in a different way, as time spent waiting.
And finally, we've only looked at the size of the surplus, not at how it's divided between buyers and sellers. That's the subject of the article on bargaining and the surplus.
It's also worth knowing that the triangle formula, $\frac{1}{2} k (q^* - q)^2$, is the same as the deadweight loss of a tax, $\frac{1}{2} t^2 \frac{BS}{B + S}$, from the article on tax incidence, because a tax of $t$ cuts quantity by $q^* - q = t \frac{BS}{B + S}$.
Where this leaves the triangle
Economists have long debated whether the deadweight losses from market interventions are big enough to worry about, and the triangle on its own suggests they usually aren't. Once we also count who ends up with the goods, the picture changes in four ways.
First, the triangle really is small. Trade stops closest to the margin, where the gains from each trade are thinnest, so the loss from the missing trades grows with the square of the cut. A cut of d costs only d² of the total surplus, so a 10% cut costs just 1% of the gains from trade.
Second, rationing is first order. When a shortage is resolved by queues, lotteries or bureaucracy rather than by prices, the units no longer go to the buyers who value them most. That misallocation loss grows as d(1 − d), and it's bigger than the triangle for every cut under 50%. At a 10% cut, rationing costs nine times as much as the triangle.
Third, the total loss equals the cut itself. In any linear market with random rationing, the two losses add up exactly: d² + d(1 − d) = d. So a 27% shortfall in quantity loses exactly 27% of the total surplus.
And finally, the policy instrument decides the cost. Holding quantity down with a quota costs d², while holding it down with a price ceiling costs d, which is 1/d times as much. At a 10% cut, the price ceiling is ten times as costly as the quota.
