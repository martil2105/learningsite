Cartels & The Prisoner's Dilemma

Why partial collusion inflicts losses on its members, why uncommitted outsiders capture the entire gain, and how game theory dismantles agreements.

In popular imagination, corporate collusion is an easy path to riches: competing firms quietly meet in a back room, agree to raise prices, and divide up the monopoly profits.

Yet in industrial economics, cartels are notorious for being fragile, short-lived, and economically self-defeating. In 1983, economists Stephen Salant, Sheldon Switzer, and Robert Reynolds uncovered a startling mathematical paradox: unless a cartel encompasses almost every firm in the industry, colluding actually reduces members' profits.

Why does coordinating output backfire? And why is the temptation to cheat on an agreement so mathematically overwhelming?

The Cartel Paradox

Imagine an industry with 5 identical competing firms. Market demand is P = 100 − Q, and each unit costs $10 to produce. In standard competition, each firm produces 15 units, the price is $25, and each earns $225 in profit.

Two of the five firms decide to join forces, forming a 2-firm cartel to restrict their combined output and elevate the market price. What happens to the profit earned by each of the two cartel members?

To see the mechanism clearly, consider an industry governed by Cournot quantity competition. Market demand is linear: $P(Q) = a - Q$, and all firms share identical constant marginal cost $c$.

MODEL ASSUMPTIONS & REAL-WORLD COLLUSION

The static Cournot framework isolates the pure non-cooperative incentives of collusion, but differs from repeated market dynamics:

Static vs repeated interaction: In reality, firms interact repeatedly over time. Repeated games permit "grim trigger" or "tit-for-tat" strategies where threats of future price wars can sustain collusion if discount factors are sufficiently high (Friedman 1971).

Symmetric cost structures: We assume all firms share identical marginal costs $c$. If one firm has significantly lower costs, it often prefers aggressive competition to collusive volume restrictions.

Price vs quantity competition: Under Bertrand price competition, any price above marginal cost is instantly undercut unless capacity constraints bind.

CONCLUSION

Cartels are often imagined as omnipotent conspiracies that quietly dictate prices. In reality, non-cooperative game theory exposes why cartels are fragile, self-sabotaging institutions:

The Merger Paradox: Unless a cartel commands an overwhelming majority of the market, restricting output benefits outsiders far more than members. When a cartel cuts production, uncommitted rivals expand along their reaction curves, capturing market share and suppressing the price increase.

The Outsider Identity: In any industry with k colluding firms, an outside firm earns exactly k times the profit of a cartel member. The larger the cartel, the greater the reward for refusing to join.

The Prisoner's Dilemma of Collusion: Every cartel member knows that if it quietly cheats on its quota or walks away, its profits will multiply. In the absence of binding, legally enforceable contracts—which antitrust laws strictly forbid—the dominant strategy is defection.

Modern Antitrust: Competition authorities do not need to patrol every warehouse. Corporate leniency programs—which grant full immunity to the first conspirator to confess—exploit this exact prisoner's dilemma, triggering an irresistible race to the courthouse.

CARTELS AND THE PRISONER'S DILEMMA

When a few firms agree to make less, the price goes up. As we'll see, the firms that stay out of the agreement gain the most, and the members can end up earning less than before.

Martin Le, September 2026

Let's say we run one of five firms that all make the same product. Each week, every firm decides how much to make, and the price settles wherever buyers will take the lot. If we could agree with the other four to make less, the price would rise and we'd all share something close to a monopoly's profit. An agreement like that is called a cartel. Competition law forbids it in most places, but let's set the law aside for now and ask a simpler question: would it even pay?

First courses usually tell the cartel story as a prisoner's dilemma, a game in which the players would all do better if they cooperated, but each one does better still by breaking ranks. Each member gains by quietly making more than its quota, even though they'd all do better if every member kept to it, and so cartels tend to fall apart. That story is right as far as it goes. In 1983, Stephen Salant, Sheldon Switzer and Robert Reynolds pointed out a problem that comes even before anyone cheats. In a simple model of competition, a cartel doesn't pay its members unless it includes almost every firm in the market.

Why would joining forces backfire, and why is staying out so tempting? Before we build the model, let's have a guess. What do you think happens to the two firms below?

What this costs you

Our model is deliberately simple, and three of its simplifications matter for real cartels.

First, our firms meet only once. Real firms compete week after week, and that changes the picture. When the game repeats, the threat of a price war can hold a cartel together, as long as the firms care enough about their future profits (Friedman, 1971). So our model shows why a cartel is fragile, not that it can't last.

Second, all our firms have the same costs. If one firm can make the product much more cheaply than the rest, it often does better by competing hard than by holding back its output in a cartel.

And finally, our firms choose quantities. If they chose prices instead, which is called Bertrand competition, a firm could take the whole market by pricing a little below the rest. Any price above cost would then be undercut straight away, unless the firms can't make enough to serve everyone.

Where this leaves cartels

The usual story says cartels fall apart because their members cheat, and that's part of the answer. Our model adds something that comes first. When a group of firms makes less, the firms outside it make more, so the cartel pays for the higher price and the outsiders gain more from it than the members do. Unless the cartel takes in almost the whole market, its members can end up no better off than before, or worse.

It also shows why staying out is so tempting, and that's the prisoner's dilemma in our title: every firm would do well in a cartel of all of them, and each does better outside one. Competition authorities use the same logic against real cartels. A leniency programme offers immunity to the first member that reports its cartel, which gives every member a reason to come forward before the others do.

Tragedy of the Commons

Why unpriced open access destroys wealth, why the second entrant is mathematically the most damaging, and how corrective pricing restores the social optimum.

Consider an open pasture, an ocean fishery, an underground water aquifer, or a congested highway. When valuable economic resources lack exclusive property rights, they are governed by open access: anyone who invests private labor or capital is free to extract what they can.

In 1954, Canadian economist H. Scott Gordon published a landmark paper revealing why open access relentlessly impoverishes communities. Left to decentralized extraction, users do not merely harvest the resource; they compete away the entire economic rent, leaving society with over-harvested ecosystems and zero net economic surplus.

How fast does this wealth vanish? And who is responsible for the lion's share of the damage?

MODEL LIMITS & OSTROM'S INSIGHT

The pure open-access model highlights non-cooperative failure, but has clear institutional boundaries:

The open-access assumption: Hardin assumed commons are unmanaged "free-for-alls". As Nobel laureate Elinor Ostrom proved empirically, real communities frequently develop intricate informal norms, monitoring systems, and harvest calendars that prevent over-exploitation without central state taxes.

Static stock vs biological regeneration: Here we model a static production function $F(E)$. In dynamic fisheries, over-fishing reduces the breeding biomass, potentially causing sudden population collapse rather than smooth rent dissipation.

CONCLUSION

The "tragedy of the commons" is often treated as a vague moral tale about human greed. Mathematical game theory reveals it to be a precise, structural failure of decentralized property rights:

The Average vs. Marginal Wedge: Under open access, individuals expand their activity as long as the resource's average product exceeds their private opportunity cost. But the socially efficient stopping point requires equating cost to marginal product.

Exact Quadratic Dissipation: In standard diminishing-returns environments, the fraction of economic wealth destroyed is exactly ((n − 1)/n)2. Total effort overshoots by ((2n − 1)/n)2, expanding toward four times the socially optimal level.

Destruction is Heavily Front-Loaded: You do not need a crowded metropolis to ruin a common pool. The single most destructive entrant is the second one, wiping out 25% of the resource's potential wealth. By user 3.4, half the rent is gone forever.

Pigouvian Restoration: When property rights cannot be assigned, a corrective Pigouvian access fee equal to AP(E*) − MP(E*) precisely closes the wedge, forcing every user to pay for their congestion footprint and resurrecting the social optimum.

TRAGEDY OF THE COMMONS

When anyone can fish a lake, boats keep coming after the extra fish have stopped paying for their time. As we'll see, most of the lake's value is gone by the time a few boats share it, and the second boat does the most damage.

Martin Le, September 2026

Let's say we fish a lake that nobody owns. Anyone can row out and fish it, and the only cost of an hour on the water is what we could have earned doing something else. A resource that anyone can use without paying is called open access, and lots of things work this way: a common pasture, an ocean fishery, an underground aquifer or a busy road.

In 1954, the economist H. Scott Gordon showed what open access does to a fishery. If nobody owns the lake, boats keep coming until an hour of fishing is worth no more than an hour of work elsewhere. The value of the catch over and above what the fishers' time is worth is called the lake's rent, and open access competes all of it away. Garrett Hardin later called this kind of problem the tragedy of the commons.

The story is usually told about crowds, and a crowd does use up the whole rent. What's less often said is how fast the rent goes. As we'll see, most of it is gone once a few boats share our lake, and the second boat does more damage than any boat after it.

Our lake with one owner

Our lake has θ = 0.5, which makes the catch grow like a square root, and A = 100. Each fish sells for £1, and an hour of a fisher's time costs w = £1, which is what they could earn elsewhere.

Let's suppose first that our lake has one owner. The owner wants the biggest rent, R(E) = F(E) − wE, so they keep adding hours as long as the next hour catches more than it costs. That means they stop where the marginal product falls to the wage, which for our lake is at E* = 2,500 hours. The catch is worth £5,000, the time costs £2,500, and the lake earns a rent of £2,500.

Each boat counts the fish it gets and ignores the fish the other boat loses. That's a congestion externality, a cost that one user imposes on another without paying for it.

What this costs you

Our lake is deliberately simple, and three of its simplifications matter.

First, we've assumed that nobody manages the lake. Hardin treated commons as free-for-alls, but Elinor Ostrom documented many real communities that look after shared resources well, with their own rules, monitoring and fishing seasons, and without a state fee or a private owner.

Second, our catch depends only on the hours fished. In a real fishery, fishing too hard shrinks the breeding stock, so the damage carries into later years and can end in a sudden collapse of the fish population rather than a smooth loss of rent.

And finally, a fee only works if whoever sets it knows the lake: the shape of the catch, the wage and, when there are only a few boats, how many there are.

Where this leaves the commons

The tragedy of the commons is often told as a story about greed, or about crowds. Our lake tells it as a question of what each user counts. An owner stops where the last hour's catch pays for its time. A boat on a shared lake counts mostly the average catch, which stays above the wage long after the owner would have stopped. So the boat keeps fishing after the extra hours have stopped paying for the lake as a whole.

That's also why a few users do so much of the damage. Most of the rent is gone before the lake is anything like crowded, so if a shared resource needs rules or a fee, it needs them early, while there are still only a few people using it.

Gini & The Lorenz Curve

From United Nations development reports to World Bank dashboards, one metric dominates conversations about economic fairness: the Gini coefficient. We are told that a nation with a Gini of 0.25 is egalitarian, while a nation with 0.50 is deeply unequal.

The Gini coefficient is the world's most widely cited measure of inequality. Yet treating a rich multi-dimensional distribution as a single scalar summary number carries profound risks:

An Intuitive Physical Meaning: The Gini is literally the expected income difference between any two randomly drawn citizens, divided by twice the society's average income.

The Crossing Trap: Two economies can share the exact same Gini coefficient while experiencing completely different realities. A society with extreme poverty at the bottom and a society with extreme billionaire wealth at the top can produce identical Gini scores whose Lorenz curves cross.

The Moral Tradeoff: When curves cross, rankings invert. Under the Atkinson index, varying inequality aversion ε from low (curbing top fortunes) to high (eradicating poverty) flips which society is judged more unequal.

The Machine Learning Bridge: In data science and credit risk, the Accuracy Ratio AR = 2·AUC − 1 is the exact same Gini construction. Evaluating classification models purely by overall Gini blinds practitioners to massive differences at specific decision thresholds.

GINI AND THE LORENZ CURVE

The Gini coefficient squeezes a whole distribution into one number. As we'll see, two societies, or two credit scorecards, can share that number and still differ in the places we care about most.

Martin Le, September 2026

Let's say we've been asked to compare two countries, and we're allowed one number each to describe how unequal their incomes are. Most of us would reach for the Gini coefficient, which runs from 0, when everyone earns the same, to 1, when one person earns everything. It turns up in development reports, statistics releases and newspaper tables, and that's no surprise, because a single number is easy to compare.

Squeezing a whole distribution into one number has to lose something, though. In this article, we'll work out what the Gini actually measures, and then build two societies that share a Gini while their poorest people live quite differently. Finally, we'll take the same idea into a bank, where a Gini is used to grade credit scorecards, and find the same blind spot there.

What the Gini measures

Most first courses introduce the Gini through a picture. We line everyone up from poorest to richest, and for each share p of the population, we plot the share of total income, L(p), that the poorest p of people receive. That's the Lorenz curve, which Max Lorenz drew in 1905. If everyone earned the same, it would be the 45-degree line, which we call the line of equality. The more unequal incomes are, the further the curve sags below it.

Let's call the area between the line of equality and the Lorenz curve A, and the area under the Lorenz curve B. The Gini is A as a share of the whole triangle under the line:

That's a tidy definition, but it doesn't say much about people. Corrado Gini's own version, from 1912, does. Let's pick two people at random and note the gap between their incomes. If we average that gap over every possible pair and divide by twice the mean income μ, we get the Gini again:

So a Gini of 0.4 means that two people picked at random differ, on average, by 80% of the mean income. There's a third route too. The Gini is twice the covariance between each person's income and their rank, divided by n times the mean, which makes it a measure of how closely income climbs with rank.

Choosing whose incomes count most

The Gini has a second blind spot, and it's at the very top. What this costs you

Each of our examples is built to make one point cleanly, and three of the simplifications are worth knowing about.

First, our two societies have only two incomes each, which makes the crossing easy to see and easy to check. Real income distributions are smooth, and a crossing between their Lorenz curves can be hard to spot by eye. That's one more reason to report a few percentile ratios, such as P90/P10 and P90/P50, and the top income shares alongside the Gini.

Second, a pure Pareto and a pure lognormal are both idealisations, picked here to have the same Gini. How much top shares differ in real data depends on how heavy the real upper tail is. It also matters what we measure: income Ginis mostly sit between about 0.3 and 0.45, while wealth Ginis often exceed 0.75.

And finally, our scorecards are stylised. A default rate of 25% is far higher than most loan books see, and B is an extreme design that's perfect on a slice of the book and no better than chance on the rest. Real scorecards sit somewhere in between, so the size of the gap will differ from ours. The crossing won't go away, though, because two different CAP curves with the same Gini always cross. That's why we should compare scorecards at the cut-off we'll actually use.

What one number can and can't tell us

The Gini is a fair summary of a whole distribution, and it has a friendly meaning: the average gap between two people, over twice the mean income. What it can't tell us is where the inequality sits. When two Lorenz curves cross, any ranking of them rests on a choice about whose incomes count most, and the Gini makes that choice for us, by weighting people according to their rank.

The same goes for a bank. A scorecard's Gini is built the same way, with defaulters in place of income, so two scorecards with the same Gini can behave quite differently at the cut-off where we actually make decisions. So when you're handed a Gini, whether it belongs to a country or to a model, it's worth asking to see the curve behind it.

What this costs you

An island with three firms leaves out most of what makes real national accounts hard, and a few of the omissions matter for the argument.

First, everything here is in euros of the same year. Real GDP is measured at constant prices, and in chain-weighted accounts like America's the components measured that way don't add up to the total, so statisticians publish specially constructed contributions to growth instead. Those are still built from the same five lines, trade included. Second, the counterfactual is the whole question. The accounts record that imports rose; whether they replaced home production, filled a gap nobody else could, or went into stock is a claim about behaviour, and answering it needs a model of demand, not a better table. Third, counting spending net of its own imports needs to know how much foreign content is inside each kind of spending, which statisticians estimate from input–output tables that arrive years late. And finally, in real data the three approaches don't agree exactly, because they're measured from different surveys, and the gap between them is published as a statistical discrepancy.

None of that rescues the reading of the minus sign as a cost. It's a correction for foreign content that the other four terms already counted, so an import that replaces nothing leaves GDP where it was.

Where this leaves the formula

The three ways of measuring GDP agree because they're three ways of adding up the same ledger, and adding up sales overcounts by an amount that depends on how firms are organised rather than on what they make. The spending formula is correct, but its minus sign is doing bookkeeping, not economics. An import changes GDP only through the home production it displaces, anywhere from none of its value to all of it, and the trade line in a GDP release reads the same across that whole range.

So when a release says that trade subtracted a point from growth, the useful question is what the imports replaced, and the answer is in the other lines of the table, not in that one.

An island with three firms leaves out most of what makes real national accounts hard, and four of the things we've left out matter for our argument.

First, everything here is in euros of the same year. Real GDP is measured at constant prices, and in chain-weighted accounts like America's, the components measured that way don't add up to the total. So statisticians publish specially constructed contributions to growth instead, and those are still built from the same five lines, trade included.

Second, the counterfactual is the whole question. The accounts record that imports rose, but not whether they replaced home production, filled a gap nobody else could, or went into stock. That's a claim about behaviour, and answering it needs a model of demand, not a better table.

Third, counting spending net of its own imports means knowing how much foreign content is inside each kind of spending. Statisticians estimate that from input–output tables, which arrive years late.

And finally, in real data the three approaches don't agree exactly, because they're measured from different surveys. The gap between them is published as a statistical discrepancy.

None of that rescues the reading of the minus sign as a cost. It's a correction for foreign content that the other four terms have already counted, so an import that replaces nothing leaves GDP where it was.

Where this leaves the formula

The spending formula is right, and so is its minus sign, as long as we read the sign as bookkeeping rather than economics. An import changes GDP only through the home production it displaces. That can be anywhere from none of its value to all of it, and the trade line in a release reads the same across that whole range.

So the next time we read that trade subtracted a point from growth, the useful question is what the imports replaced. The answer is in the other lines of the table, not in that one.

Let's say that last year you spent a fifth of your budget on energy, for heating, electricity and fuel, and the other four-fifths on everything else. This year the price of energy doubles, and nothing else changes price. Here's a question that sounds as if it should have one answer: how much more income would you need this year to be exactly as well off as you were last year?

The obvious answer is 20%, because that's what it would cost to buy last year's shopping again. Take a moment to make your own guess before we look at it more carefully.

The right answer depends on something about you that the question didn't mention, which is how readily you'd switch away from energy now that it costs more. Economists measure that with the elasticity of substitution, written 𝜎 σ, which says how much the ratio of what you buy of two goods changes when their relative price changes. At 𝜎 σ = 0 you never switch, so you need the full 20%. At 𝜎 σ = 1, which is the case where you always spend the same share of your budget on each good, you need 14.9%. At 𝜎 σ = 2 you need only 11.1%. The amount you'd need is called the true cost-of-living index, and the 20% from buying last year's shopping again is only right for someone who never switches.

That's the textbook's lesson about price indices, and it's usually stated in one line: a fixed basket overstates the cost of living, because people substitute away from what got dearer. It's true, and in the rest of this article we'll see exactly how big the overstatement is, why it's often small, and a way around it that doesn't need to know 𝜎 σ at all.

If you set energy's multiplier back to 1 in the lab, every index agrees, whatever 𝜎 σ is. The overstatement comes entirely from prices moving apart, and it turns out to have a simple size. An answer that doesn't need σ

Now for the dashed lines in the same figure. They belong to the Fisher index, which is simply the geometric mean of the Laspeyres and Paasche indices, so it can be computed from prices and the two baskets actually bought, with no knowledge of 𝜎 σ. Its error is far smaller: when energy's price rises by 5% and 𝜎 σ is 1, it's about a hundred times smaller than the fixed basket's. Its lines also have a slope of 3 rather than 2, so it shrinks eight times over when the shock is halved, and the advantage grows as shocks get smaller.

You can see the same thing in the lab. With energy doubling and 𝜎 σ = 1, Fisher says 15.5% against the true 14.9%, which closes 88% of the fixed basket's gap. And if you drag 𝜎 σ anywhere between 0 and 3, the Fisher bar follows the truth, because the household's new basket carries the information about how it switched. Fisher is exact when 𝜎 σ = 0, since the two baskets are then the same.

Why does averaging two wrong answers give a nearly right one? For small price changes, the Laspeyres and Paasche errors are almost exactly equal and opposite, and a geometric mean cancels the equal parts. What's left over is the difference between the two errors, which is a whole order smaller. Indices with this property, which approximate the true index to second order for any smooth homothetic preferences, are called superlative, a term due to Erwin Diewert, and Fisher and the closely related Törnqvist index are the two in common use.

Chaining, and where it breaks

The other fix statisticians use is to update the basket often. Instead of comparing this December with last December, they compare each month with the one before and multiply the monthly changes together, which is called chaining. When energy doubles in twelve equal monthly steps, chaining brings the fixed basket down from 20% to 15.2%, and chained Fisher lands within a few thousandths of a point of the true 14.9%. Each month's price change is small, and the square law makes each month's error smaller still.

But chaining has a failure of its own, and you can see it by switching the figure to a good that goes a quarter off every other month and makes up a fifth of the budget. Prices return exactly to where they started every two months, so the true index does too. The chained fixed basket doesn't. In a sale month the household's basket leans towards the cheap good, and valuing that basket at the full price the next month registers a rise that's bigger than the fall that preceded it. What this costs you

Two numbers per town is a very small model of a labour market, and a few of the things it leaves out matter here.

First, people also move in and out of the labour force, and in real data those flows are as large as the ones between work and unemployment, so a proper accounting uses three states rather than two. Second, everybody here has the same chance of finding a job however long they've been looking. In practice the chance falls the longer someone is unemployed, which makes long-term unemployment more common than these formulas say. Third, the flows are measured once a month, so someone who loses a job and finds another between two surveys never shows up at all, and correcting for that makes both measured rates larger. COMPOUND GROWTH

A one-point gap in growth rates doubles the gap in living standards in about seventy years. But the average of an economy's growth rates isn't the rate it grew at, and as we'll see, once growth is uncertain the typical path falls behind the expected one for ever.

Martin Le, September 2026

Let's start with two economies that begin the century equally rich. One grows by 2% a year and the other by 3%. That's a small difference, the sort that gets lost in a single year's statistical revisions, and yet after a hundred years the first economy is 7.2 times as rich as it was and the second is 19.2 times as rich, so the second is more than two and a half times as rich as the first.

That's compound growth: each year's growth applies to everything the earlier years produced, so the economy grows by the same percentage of an ever larger amount. On an ordinary axis it looks like acceleration. On a logarithmic axis, where each step up multiplies rather than adds, constant growth is a straight line, and the steepness of the line is the growth rate. Switch between the two views below.

On the logarithmic axis, the two straight lines move apart at a constant rate, which means the ratio between the two economies grows like an economy of its own, at about one percentage point a year. That's where the most quoted rule in growth economics comes from, the rule of 70: something growing at g% a year doubles in about 70 / g years.

𝑇 2 = ln ⁡ 2 ln ⁡ ( 1 + 𝑔 ) ≈ 70 100 𝑔 T 2 ​ = ln(1+g) ln2 ​ ≈ 100g 70 ​

The rule is exact at a growth rate of 1.98%, and at 2% the exact answer is 35.0 years. It drifts at higher rates, which is why bankers use 72 instead, since the rule of 72 is exact at 7.85%. Applied to the gap between our two economies, it says the richer one's lead doubles every 71.0 years. So a single percentage point, sustained, is the difference between two economies that end the century at equal living standards and two that end it with one more than twice as rich as the other.

That's the standard lesson, and it's right. But it quietly assumes that growth is steady, and real growth isn't. So here's a question about an economy whose growth goes up and down.

Economy A grows 3% every year. Economy B grows 8% one year and shrinks 2% the next, over and over, which also averages 3% a year. After 50 years, which is richer?

The average isn't the rate you grew at

A year of 8% growth followed by a year of 2% decline multiplies income by 1.08 × 0.98, which is 1.0584, not 1.06. So two years of B grow the economy by less than two years of A, even though the growth rates average the same 3%. The rate B actually grew at, called its compound annual growth rate, is 2.88%, and over fifty years the missing tenth of a point adds up to a 6.1% difference in living standards.

The simplest version of this is familiar to anyone who has watched a share price: a 10% fall followed by a 10% rise leaves you 1% worse off, not back where you started. When growth is uncertain

Alternating booms and busts are a toy. A better model makes each year's growth random: independent from year to year, with a spread measured by 𝜎 σ, the standard deviation of the logarithm of the growth factor. At a volatility of 2%, roughly what a country's GDP growth shows, the median economy ends up 2.66 times as rich and nearly half of the paths finish above the expected level. This difference between the average across possible outcomes and what happens along one path over time is the subject of what Ole Peters has called the ergodicity problem in economics. The practical upshot is a question worth asking of any forecast: is it a statement about the average of many outcomes, or about the one you're actually going to live through?

The maths

A quantity growing at a constant rate g doubles in ln 2 / ln(1 + g) years, and since ln 2 is 0.693 and ln(1 + g) is a little less than g, the right numerator for small rates is a little above 69, which is where 70 comes from. After t years, the log of the level is normal with mean μt and standard deviation 𝜎 σ√t, and the expected level sits ½ 𝜎 σ√t standard deviations above the median, which gives the share of paths above it.

What this costs you

The lab makes growth independent from year to year and lognormal, and neither is quite true of economies.

First, GDP tends to bounce back after a recession, at least partly, so bad years are followed by better-than-average ones more often than independence allows, and that reduces the drag. Second, real growth has fatter tails than a normal distribution, with rare collapses that are bigger than the model expects, and that increases it. Third, the volatility itself has to be estimated, usually from a few decades of data, which is a short sample for a number whose square matters. And finally, the expected level isn't wrong, just often irrelevant. It's the right number for the total of many independent paths added together, which is exactly why an insurer or a diversified investor can use it, and the wrong number for any one of them.

Where this leaves the average

Compound growth turns small differences in growth rates into large differences in living standards, and a one-point gap doubles the gap between two economies in about seventy years. What the rule assumes is steady growth. With swings, the rate an economy actually grows at is the average growth rate minus roughly half the variance, and with uncertain growth the expected path and the typical one drift apart for ever, at exactly that rate. For a whole country the difference is small. For a household, a firm or a portfolio, it can be the whole story.

COMPOUND GROWTH

A one-point gap in growth rates doubles the gap in living standards in about seventy years. But the average of an economy's growth rates isn't the rate it grew at, and as we'll see, once growth is uncertain the typical path falls behind the expected one for ever.

Martin Le, September 2026

Let's take two economies that start the century equally rich. Our first economy grows by 2% a year and our second by 3%. That's a small difference, the sort that can get lost in a single year's statistical revisions. Yet after a hundred years, the first economy is 7.2 times as rich as it was, and the second is 19.2 times as rich. So by the end, the second economy is more than two and a half times as rich as the first.

That's compound growth: each year's growth applies to everything the earlier years produced, so our economies grow by the same percentage of an ever larger amount. On an ordinary axis, that looks like acceleration. On a logarithmic axis, where each step up multiplies rather than adds, constant growth is a straight line, and the steepness of the line is the growth rate. Switch between the two views below, and drag the year to follow our two economies.

On the logarithmic axis, our two straight lines move apart at a constant rate. That means the ratio between our two economies grows like an economy of its own, at about one percentage point a year. It's also where the most quoted rule in growth economics comes from, the rule of 70: something growing at g% a year doubles in about 70 / g years.

If we check the rule, it's exact at a growth rate of 1.98%, and at 2% the exact answer is 35.0 years, which is where you'll see the rings on the chart. At higher rates the rule drifts, which is why bankers use 72 instead, since the rule of 72 is exact at 7.85%. If we apply the exact formula to the gap between our two economies, the richer one's lead doubles every 71.0 years. So a single percentage point, sustained for a century, leaves one economy more than twice as rich as the other.

That's the standard lesson, and it's right. But it quietly assumes that growth is steady, and real growth isn't. So let's try a question about an economy whose growth goes up and down.

Economy A grows 3% every year. Economy B grows 8% one year and shrinks 2% the next, over and over, which also averages 3% a year. After 50 years, which is richer?

The average isn't the rate you grew at

Let's look at two years of B. A year of 8% growth followed by a year of 2% decline multiplies income by 1.08 × 0.98, which is 1.0584. Two years of A multiply it by 1.03 × 1.03, which is 1.0609. So two years of B grow our economy by less than two years of A, even though the growth rates average the same 3%.

The rate B actually grew at, called its compound annual growth rate, is 2.88%. Over fifty years, the missing tenth of a point adds up to a 6.1% difference in living standards.

You may have met the simplest version of this with a share price: a 10% fall followed by a 10% rise leaves you 1% worse off, not back where you started. That's because a fall takes a bigger bite out of a larger number than the same rise adds back to a smaller one. When growth is uncertain

Alternating booms and busts are a toy, so let's make each year's growth random instead. This gap between the average across possible outcomes and what happens along one path over time is the subject of what Ole Peters has called the ergodicity problem in economics. It leaves us with a useful question to ask of any forecast. Is it a statement about the average of many outcomes, or about the one you're actually going to live through?

The maths

Let's collect the formulas. A quantity growing at a constant rate g doubles in ln 2 / ln(1 + g) years. Since ln 2 is 0.693 and ln(1 + g) is a little less than g, the right numerator for small rates is a little above 69, which is where 70 comes from.

After t years, the log of the level is normal with mean μt and standard deviation 𝜎 σ√t. The expected level sits ½ 𝜎 σ√t standard deviations above the median, and that gives us the share of paths above it.

What this costs you

Our lab makes growth independent from year to year and lognormal, and neither is quite true of economies.

First, GDP tends to bounce back after a recession, at least partly. So bad years are followed by better-than-average ones more often than independence allows, and that reduces the drag.

Second, real growth has fatter tails than a normal distribution, with rare collapses that are bigger than our model expects, and that increases the drag.

Third, we have to estimate the volatility itself, usually from a few decades of data. That's a short sample for a number whose square matters.

And finally, the expected level isn't wrong, just often irrelevant. It's the right number for the total of many independent paths added together, which is why an insurer or a diversified investor can use it. It's the wrong number for any one of those paths.

Where this leaves the average

Compound growth turns small differences in growth rates into large differences in living standards, and the rule of 70 is a good guide to how quickly. What the rule asks us to assume, though, is that growth is steady.

Once growth swings, the rate an economy actually grows at falls below its average growth rate by roughly half the variance, and the expected path and the typical one drift apart for ever. For a whole country, that gap is small. For a household, a firm or a portfolio, it can be the whole story.

MONEY CREATION

Every banker knows a bank needs deposits before it can lend, and the textbook says the central bank's reserves get multiplied into money. As we'll see, the first is true of one bank lending alone and false of the banking system, and the second is a ratio that holds whichever way the causation runs.

Martin Le, September 2026

Ask a banker where the money for a loan comes from and you'll usually get the answer that a bank lends out its depositors' money, and so needs deposits before it can lend. Ask a textbook where money comes from and you'll often get a second story: the central bank supplies reserves, and banks lend them out again and again until each euro of reserves has become several euros of money, a process called the money multiplier.

Both stories describe something real, but not the thing they're usually taken to describe. The quickest way to see why is to follow a single loan through the books. Let's take an island with three banks, called Anchor, Birch and Cedar, holding half, 30% and 20% of the island's deposits, and a central bank where each of them keeps an account. The balance in that account is called reserves, and it's what banks use to pay each other.

Three banks before anything happens. Anchor holds half of the island's deposits, Birch 30% and Cedar 20%, and each keeps a tenth of its deposits as reserves at the central bank.

Where the loan's money comes from

Step through the first move. When Anchor lends €100 to a bakery, it doesn't take €100 from anyone's account. It writes two entries at once, a €100 loan among its assets and a €100 deposit for the bakery among its liabilities, and that's all a loan is. Nobody else's deposit is smaller, so the island has €100 more money than it did a moment ago. Money held in bank accounts is created when banks lend, and destroyed when loans are repaid.

Then the bakery spends the money. Its suppliers bank all over the island, and since Anchor holds half the deposits, about half the payments come back to Anchor and half go to Birch and Cedar. Every payment that leaves Anchor has to be settled in reserves, so Anchor loses €50 of reserves, which in this example is every reserve it had. That's the banker's story, and it's completely right from where the banker sits. A bank that lends alone loses most of the loan in reserves and has to replace them, by attracting deposits or by borrowing, so from the inside it looks as if deposits have to come first.

Lending in step

Now take the last two steps. Birch and Cedar lend too, €60 and €40, in proportion to their size, and their borrowers spend the money the same way the bakery did. Payments now flow in every direction, and they cancel. Every bank ends with exactly the reserves it started with, and there are €200 more deposits on the island, which is exactly the €200 that was lent.

So whether a loan drains a bank's reserves depends on what the other banks are doing. The lab below makes that the control. Reserves only move between banks; the system as a whole can't lose them by lending.

John Maynard Keynes made this point in 1930 in A Treatise on Money, where he wrote that "there is no limit to the amount of bank money which the banks can safely create provided that they move forward in step". That's not a loophole. It's why the banking system doesn't need deposits in order to lend: the loans create the deposits, and when the banks move together, the reserves they pay out to each other come straight back.

So what does limit lending?

If deposits don't, something else must, and in practice it's a list of prices and rules rather than a quantity. A bank that loses reserves can borrow them from other banks or the central bank at close to the central bank's interest rate, so the interest rate is the price of lending out of step. Deposits are usually a cheaper way of holding on to reserves, which is why banks compete for them, and why the banker's story is really about cost rather than about a hard limit. Beyond that, banks need enough capital to absorb losses on their loans, and borrowers who want to borrow at the rates on offer. In the United States, the Federal Reserve set reserve requirements to zero in March 2020, so the requirement that the textbook story leans on doesn't exist there at all.

What this costs you

Three banks on an island leave out a lot of real banking, and a few of the omissions matter here.

First, payments don't land at banks exactly in proportion to their size. Neither is a statement about the banking system as a whole, where loans create deposits, banks lending in step lose no reserves at all, and the multiplier holds just as exactly when the arrow runs from loans to reserves as when it runs the other way.

You were probably taught to find it by checking each cell of a payoff matrix. But one game in eight has no cell that survives, and in those games, your own behaviour is decided entirely by the other player's payoffs. As we'll see, that's why raising a penalty doesn't reduce the thing it punishes.

Imagine a trader on a bank's desk who can either stay inside their position limit or quietly breach it. Down the corridor, a risk officer can either audit the trader's book or spend the afternoon on something else. When they make their decisions, neither of them can see what the other is doing.

This is exactly the kind of interaction that game theory was built for, and you've almost certainly been shown how to solve it. You draw the payoff matrix and go through it cell by cell. For each cell, you ask whether either player, knowing what the other just did, would rather have done something else. The cells where nobody would want to move are the Nash equilibria. It's a lovely procedure that takes about thirty seconds and needs no calculus.

So the best replies chase each other round the four cells and never land on the same one.

The natural reaction is to assume this is a trick example, but it isn't. It belongs to a category of games that the procedure simply can't handle, and most courses don't mention how big that category is.

How often the procedure comes up empty

For counting purposes, only the order of each player's payoffs matters, because every question the procedure asks is a comparison. This means a generic two-by-two game is one of 24 × 24 = 576 equally likely orderings, so the answer is an exact fraction rather than a simulation.

All 576 two-by-two games, enumerated exactly. 72 of them — one in eight — have no cell that survives.

Mean number of surviving cells: exactly 1, over every game there is

One game in eight has nothing to find. Three in four have exactly one surviving cell, and one in eight have two, which is a problem of its own, because a model that offers two answers and no way to choose between them hasn't finished predicting. If we give each player more than two actions, the empty share climbs towards 1 / e, about 37%, while the average number of surviving cells stays pinned at exactly one at every size. That isn't a coincidence, and the reason is simple. There are n² cells, and a cell needs to be the best in its column for one player and the best in its row for the other. Each of those has a chance of 1 / n, so multiplying everything together gives exactly one, however big we make the game.

What the answer looks like instead

In a game with no cell to stop at, neither player can afford to be predictable, because the other player can exploit anything you do reliably. So the equilibrium isn't a cell but a pair of frequencies: how often the trader breaches, and how often the desk audits. Game theorists call this a mixed-strategy equilibrium.

Exactly one pair of frequencies holds up. If auditing were even slightly too rare, breaching would be strictly better, so the trader would breach every time, and that would make auditing worth it. So the frequencies have to sit precisely where each player has been made indifferent. That's the whole derivation, and it's worth noticing how strange it is. Your job isn't to do well. It's to leave the other person with nothing to prefer.

What this costs you

A model this sharp is sharp because of what it leaves out, and the omissions here are real ones.

First, the model covers a single period. Nobody has a reputation, a career, a promotion to lose or a colleague to warn. Yet repetition is exactly the setting where fines recover some of their power, because a large penalty can support cooperation that a small one can't. Second, it assumes both players are risk neutral, which is a poor assumption for a fine large enough to end somebody's career, and the risk-averse version does give the penalty back some grip. Third, it assumes the trader knows the desk's costs and the desk knows the trader's gain. This common-knowledge assumption does a great deal of quiet work, and the readout result in the identity figure leans on it entirely. Fourth, it treats the risk desk as a single agent with one objective, whereas a real one has a budget set elsewhere, a headcount, and more to do than this. And finally, it assumes the fine is a pure transfer, when in practice a fine large enough to matter also destroys value.

It's also worth mentioning what the empirical literature on deterrence broadly finds, because it's not a bad match. The certainty of being caught does more work than the severity of the consequence. This model offers one mechanism for why that might be, and it's a mechanism that doesn't need anybody to be irrational or to misjudge probabilities. All it needs is for the person doing the catching to also get to decide how hard to look.

Where this leaves the procedure

Checking cells isn't wrong, and for three games in four, it gives the whole answer. What it hides is that it's a search over a restricted set, namely the outcomes where both players are predictable, and that the search comes up empty often enough to matter. When it does, the equilibrium is still there. It's just a pair of frequencies rather than a cell, and it has a property the cell version never shows you: each player's behaviour is written in the other player's numbers.

That's a reasonable lesson to take away about equilibrium in general. An equilibrium is a statement about a system, not a sum of statements about the people in it. The tempting move is to work out what each person wants and then add it all up, but that's exactly the move the arithmetic above refuses.

NASH EQUILIBRIUM

Checking each cell of a payoff matrix finds nothing in one two-by-two game in eight. As we'll see, in those games each player's behaviour is set by the other player's payoffs, so a bigger fine buys less monitoring rather than less misconduct.

Let's start on a bank's trading floor, with two people and one decision each. Our trader can either stay inside their position limit or quietly breach it. Down the corridor, our risk officer can either audit the trader's book or spend the afternoon on something else. When they decide, neither of them can see what the other is doing.

This is the kind of situation game theory was built for, and most of us have been shown how to solve it. We draw the payoff matrix and go through it one cell at a time. For each cell, we ask whether either player, knowing what the other just did, would rather have done something else. The cells where nobody wants to move are called the Nash equilibria of the game. It's a tidy procedure that takes about thirty seconds and needs no calculus.

How often the procedure comes up empty

Let's count. Every question we ask of a cell is a comparison, so only the order of each player's payoffs matters. A player's four payoffs can be put in order in 24 ways, which means a two-by-two game with no ties is one of 24 × 24 = 576 equally likely orderings. That's few enough for us to check every one, so the answer is an exact fraction rather than an estimate from a simulation.

As the game grows, the share with no surviving cell climbs towards 1 / e, about 37%. The average number of surviving cells, though, stays at one for every size, and there's a short reason for that. With n actions each, there are n² cells, and a cell survives when it's the best in its column for one player and the best in its row for the other. Each of those has a chance of 1 / n, so on average we expect n² × 1 / n × 1 / n surviving cells. That's exactly one, however big we make the game.

What the answer looks like instead

So what does an equilibrium look like when there's no cell to stop at? Neither player can afford to be predictable, because the other can exploit anything they do reliably. So the equilibrium isn't a cell but a pair of frequencies: how often our trader breaches, and how often our risk desk audits. This is called a mixed-strategy equilibrium, because each player mixes their two actions at random in fixed proportions.

Only one pair of frequencies holds up. Suppose auditing were even slightly too rare. Then breaching would be strictly better, so the trader would breach every time, and that would make auditing worth it after all. So the frequencies have to sit precisely where each player is indifferent between their two actions. That's the whole derivation, and it's worth noticing how odd it is. Nobody mixes in order to do well for themselves. Each player's mix is there to leave the other player with nothing to prefer.

Two parties, one surplus, and a frontier of deals every point of which beats no deal at all. Nothing in that picture picks a point. But write down how the offers actually alternate, and the model picks exactly one — and the split it picks is built from the other side's impatience, not your own.

Two firms can merge, and the merger is worth more than the two of them apart. Let's normalise the extra value to one pot and ask how they split it. The textbook picture is a line: every point on the line is a deal both sides prefer to walking away, and the line is called the bargaining frontier. Run your finger along it and try to find the point economics recommends. There isn't one, and that's where most courses stop.

It's a strange place to stop, because the picture is missing the only thing that actually happens at a bargaining table: offers go back and forth, and every round of delay costs both sides something. Write that down and the picture stops being silent. Let's start, though, by confirming that the silence is real.

A procedure that picks

Now suppose the two sides take turns. A opens with an offer, B accepts it or counters, and so on, one round each. Each round that passes burns a little of the surplus, which we'll write as a discount factor δ per round: what a promise of the whole pot is worth one round late. Work backwards from the deadline and the whole thing unrolls. At the last round whoever is holding the offer takes everything, because refusing leaves nothing. The strategic route we've just walked treats bargaining as a game with moves and deadlines. There's an older, axiomatic route — Nash's — that never mentions an order of play at all: it writes down properties a fair split should have, and lands on the single point that maximises a weighted product,

max ⁡ 𝑥 𝑥 1 / 𝑟 𝐴 ( 1 − 𝑥 ) 1 / 𝑟 𝐵 x max ​ x 1/r A ​ (1−x) 1/r B ​

With weights proportional to the inverse impatience rates, that product is maximised at exactly r_B/(r_A + r_B). One theory derives the answer from how offers alternate; the other derives it from axioms about fairness, and they land on the same number to eight decimal places. Rubinstein proved the equivalence in 1982, and it's the reason "bargaining power" has an operational meaning: it is impatience, measured.

The two routes agree to 2.5e-5 — a theorem, not a tuning choice.

What your fallback is worth

Everyone "knows" a better outside option improves your deal. In this model, test that: give A an outside option — a walk-away value — as a share of the surplus, and slide it up from zero. While it sits anywhere below the equilibrium share, the split does not move by a decimal. The threat to walk is already priced in at exactly zero, because B knows A prefers the deal.

Then A's fallback crosses the share, and the payoff snaps onto it: one for one, and no further. An outside option is a step function, not a lever — worthless until it binds, everything once it does. What this model costs you

First, it predicts that agreement is instant: the first offer is always accepted, so every observed strike, holdout and month of haggling is outside the model. The models that do produce delay make the pie shrink asymmetrically or the offers unobservable, and they are a literature of their own.

Second, the surplus here is money and nothing else, so the frontier is a straight line and "efficient" means "on it". With risk aversion the frontier bends, efficiency and fairness genuinely come apart, and the axiomatic route stops pinning down a unique answer.

And third, there are two parties who can find each other. The moment one side's alternative is another bargaining partner, the outside options become strategic, and the clean step function above is the best case, not the worst.

BARGAINING AND THE SURPLUS

Two firms can split the gains from a deal in any way that both prefer to no deal at all. As we'll see, once they take turns making offers, the split they settle on is set by how impatient each one is.

Martin Le, September 2026

Let's imagine two firms, A and B, that are talking about a merger. Together they'd be worth more than the two of them apart, and that extra value is the surplus from the deal. To keep our sums simple, we'll measure the surplus as one pot of size one. A deal is then just a split of the pot: A takes a share of it, and B takes the rest.

First courses in economics draw the possible deals as a line called the bargaining frontier. Every point on it splits the whole pot, so every point is a deal that both firms prefer to walking away with nothing. It's a useful picture of what's possible, but it doesn't tell us which split our two firms will agree on. They usually say that bargaining power decides it, and leave the question there.

In this article, we'll give the two firms a simple set of rules for making offers and see what those rules do. As we'll see, they pick exactly one point on the frontier, and that point is built from how impatient each firm is compared with the other. Taking turns

Here's a simple version. A makes the first offer, and B either accepts it or turns it down and makes a counter-offer one round later. Then it's A's turn to accept or counter, and so on. Waiting isn't free, because each round of delay makes the deal worth a little less to both firms. We'll measure that with a discount factor, δ, which is what the whole pot is worth to a firm if it arrives one round late. Finally, let's add a deadline. If there's no deal after T rounds, both firms walk away with nothing.

So they only need to offer the other firm what the whole pot would be worth to it a round later, which is δ, and they can keep 1 − δ. What an outside option is worth

It seems natural that a better fallback should mean a better deal. If A could walk away and get something elsewhere, surely that ought to raise A's share? Let's test it. We'll give A an outside option, a payoff that A can take instead of carrying on whenever it turns down B's offer. We'll measure it as a share of the pot, just like the split.

If you drag A's outside option in the chart below, you'll see that the blue dot doesn't move at first. As long as the option is worth less than the 75% A gets from bargaining, the split doesn't change at all. That's because A's threat to walk away isn't believable. B knows that A would rather take the deal than the outside option, so the threat adds nothing to A's share.

Once the outside option is worth more than A's share, it starts to count. B now has to offer A at least what A could get by walking away, so A's payoff rises one for one with the option. In other words, an outside option works like a floor rather than a lever. It doesn't help until it's higher than the share A would get anyway, and after that, every extra point of it goes to A.

What this costs you

Alternating offers give us a sharp answer, but it comes from a small model. Before you lean on it, it's worth knowing which three of its assumptions do most of the work.

First, the model says that the two sides agree straight away. A's first offer is always accepted, so strikes, holdouts and months of haggling can't happen here. Models that do produce delay need extra ingredients, and they're a topic of their own.

Second, the surplus here is money and nothing else, so the frontier is a straight line. If the two sides dislike risk, the frontier drawn in terms of what each side actually values bends, and our tidy formula for A's share no longer holds as it stands.

And finally, there are only two firms, and neither has anyone else to turn to. If A's outside option is really a deal with a third firm, then it comes out of another bargain, and it's no longer a fixed number we can slide along an axis.

What the rules of the game add

The bargaining frontier is a good picture of what's possible. It shows every split that both sides prefer to no deal, and first courses are right that it can't tell us which one they'll choose. What picks the point is the way the bargaining happens. Once we write down whose turn it is and what waiting costs, our model settles on a single split, and that split depends on how impatient each side is compared with the other.

That gives bargaining power a concrete meaning. In this model, it's about how much each side loses by waiting, and an outside option only adds to it once it's worth more than the deal on the table. So the next time you hear a negotiation described in terms of who holds the stronger hand, it's worth asking who can better afford to wait.

A tax, a quota and a private deal can all bring a polluter's output down to the right level, and they even move the same money. As we'll see, the choice between them only matters for efficiency once we're unsure of the costs, and then two slopes decide it.

Let's say we're in charge of regulating a stretch of river. Upstream there's a plant, and downstream there's a town that takes its drinking water from the river. Every unit the plant makes earns it some money, and it also does 12 of damage to the town's water. The town gets none of the plant's revenue and all of the damage.

The plant's books only count the costs the plant pays itself, so the market settles at 40 units. If we add the town's damage to the plant's costs, the best output for everyone together is 32. A cost like this, which falls on someone outside the deal between buyer and seller, is called an externality.

What if we don't know the costs?

Everything so far assumed that we know the whole picture: the damage, the plant's costs and what buyers will pay. The two instruments handle that uncertainty differently. A quota fixes the quantity, so when the benefit curve moves, the plant keeps making the same amount while the right output shifts. A tax fixes the price, so the plant's output moves with the benefit curve, but it can move too far or not far enough. What this costs you

Before you lean on these results, it's worth being clear about three things our small model assumes.

First, the three instruments are only equivalent when we know the costs at the moment we set them. That's rarely true, and as we've seen, once the plant's benefit curve is uncertain, the choice affects how much surplus we lose and not just who gets the money.

Second, our bargain needs two parties who can find each other and agree without it costing them anything. With a thousand households downstream, getting everyone to chip in is a problem of its own, because each household would rather the others paid.

And finally, our damage curve is a straight line, so "steep or flat" is a single number. Real damage curves can bend, with thresholds and tipping points, and a curve that suddenly gets steep makes the case for the quota stronger.

Choosing an instrument

When we know the costs, a tax, a quota and a bargain are three routes to the same output, and they even move the same sum of money. What differs is who ends up holding it: the government, the plant or the town. That's a real difference, but it's about fairness rather than efficiency.

Uncertainty changes that. Once we can't be sure of the plant's costs, the instruments stop being equivalent, and the better one is the one that lets the cheaper thing vary. If damage is flat, it costs little to let the quantity move, so a tax does better. If damage is steep, it's safer to hold the quantity still and let the price move, so a quota does better. So the next time you hear a tax and a cap argued over, it's worth asking which slope is steeper.

Martin Le, September 2026

Here's a puzzle that opens every labour-economics course. Firms pay wages above the level at which willing workers are plentiful — often well above — and the workers they hire are not obviously better than the ones waiting outside. A market clears when the price balances the two sides; this price doesn't. Either the textbook supply-and-demand story is wrong, or the wage isn't doing the job we assumed it does.

The answer this article builds is the second one, and it turns on a single observation: a wage buys effort, not hours. What the firm gets for its payroll is not headcount but attention, and effort responds to how well the job pays. So the firm's real problem is to buy effort cheaply — and we can put that whole problem on one curve.

So why the queue?

At the tangency wage, willing workers outnumber jobs. That's not a failure of the model — it's the model's engine. In the monitoring reading, a worker who shirks is caught with probability p and loses a job worth a premium over the fallback; the wage has to be high enough that getting caught hurts. and the people waiting outside are what makes dismissal real: if the queue vanished, the fired worker would be rehired by lunchtime and the premium would buy nothing. The queue is the collateral behind the wage.

At the tangency wage of 20, the firms' staffing budget hires 60 of the 100 — and the 40 waiting outside are not a disequilibrium: they are what the wage is buying.

Cut the wage and the queue shrinks, but so does the effort each employee buys — and the firm is not trying to clear this market. It is trying to buy effort cheaply, and the unemployed are part of the price.

Monitoring, penalties, and what actually collapses

Only the product of the catching probability and the penalty matters, so they are the same lever on a hyperbola — the same arithmetic this catalogue met in the audit-rate article, where raising the penalty bought less monitoring rather than more compliance. Here be precise about the collapse everyone quotes: perfect monitoring alone does not push the premium to zero. At p = 1 with a bounded penalty the premium is still 100%; the construction collapses only as the product pF grows without bound.

The premium above the reservation wage is exactly 1/(pF) of it: 2000% at p = 0.05, 500% at 0.2, 100% at p = F = 1. Monitoring and the penalty are the same lever, and only the product matters.

What this diagram costs you

First, one firm, one period, effort as a single number. Real effort has dimensions — care, speed, honesty — and real firms also use bonds, promotions and pride; the measured effort responses to wage premia are far gentler than this curve implies.

Second, the unemployed here queue quietly and cost nothing. Once they search, the queue's length feeds back into the reservation wage, and the clean closed forms gain a labour-market block.

And third, the fairness reading — people work harder when they feel paid fairly — produces the same above-market wages from different machinery, and the two explanations are still being told apart in the data.

A firm that pays more than it has to might be buying effort as well as hours. As we'll see, the cheapest effort sits at a wage set by the effort curve alone, and the queue of people outside is part of how that works.

Martin Le, September 2026

Let's say we run a firm and we're deciding what to pay our staff. Plenty of people would take the job at a wage of 10, which we'll call the reservation wage, the lowest pay at which someone is willing to work. So why would we ever pay more than that?

The simplest supply-and-demand story says we shouldn't. If more people want the job than there are jobs, the wage should fall until the two sides balance. Yet many firms do pay more than the lowest wage they could hire at, and the people they hire aren't obviously better than the ones who'd take less. Either the firms are making a mistake, or the wage is doing a job that the simple story leaves out.

In this article, we'll follow the second idea. A wage doesn't only buy hours, it also buys effort, how hard people work while they're there, and effort depends on how well the job pays. So our real problem is to buy effort as cheaply as we can. As we'll see, that problem fits on a single curve, and the number of workers we want never enters it.

You probably found it where the dashed line just touches the curve without cutting through it. That's the steepest line from the origin that still reaches the curve, so it's where effort is cheapest. A lower wage would shorten the queue, but it would also buy less effort from each worker, so the firms aren't trying to clear this market.

To see why, let's look at one well-known version of the story, where the problem is that we can't watch everyone all the time. A worker weighs what slacking off would gain them against the risk of being caught. This is where the queue comes in. Losing the job only hurts because there isn't another one like it waiting. If there were no queue, a worker who was caught and fired could walk straight into a new job at the same pay, and the threat would lose its bite. So the people waiting outside are what gives the premium its force.

Watching more closely, or punishing harder

Only the product pF appears in our formula, so for the premium it doesn't matter whether we catch slackers more often or punish them harder. Doubling one has the same effect as doubling the other. A similar trade-off came up in our article on Nash equilibrium, where raising a penalty bought less monitoring rather than less misconduct.

Notice what happens at p = 1, too. In our version, p is a probability, so it can't go above one, and even with perfect monitoring the premium is still 100% as long as the penalty stays at F = 1. What this costs you

Our model is small, and three of its simplifications are worth keeping in mind before you use it.

First, there's one firm, one period and a single number for effort. Real effort has several sides, such as care, speed and honesty, and real firms also motivate people with promotions, bonuses and pride. Nothing guarantees that real effort responds to pay as strongly as our curve does.

Second, the people in our queue just wait. In a fuller model, the length of the queue affects how quickly a fired worker can find a new job, which feeds back into the reservation wage, and our tidy formulas pick up a whole labour market.

And finally, there's another story that gives a similar result. If people work harder when they feel fairly paid, firms also end up paying more than the lowest wage they could hire at, for different reasons. It isn't easy to tell the two stories apart in the data.

Why the wage stays high

Once a wage buys effort as well as hours, the firm's problem changes. It's no longer looking for the lowest wage anyone will accept, but for the cheapest effort, and that sits where a 1% pay rise buys 1% more effort. How many workers the firm wants doesn't come into it.

That turns the usual picture around. The people who'd work for less aren't a sign that the market has failed to clear. In the monitoring story, they're what gives the higher wage its force, because losing a well-paid job only hurts when there isn't another one waiting. So when you see a queue for jobs that pay well, it's worth asking what the wage is buying.
