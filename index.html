The Economics Sequence

An ordered progression through microeconomics and macroeconomics, in curriculum order. Each article builds on the machinery of the last, connected by a two-sentence bridge explaining what the previous article established and what the next one needs.

Introduction

Trade allows two economies to mutually gain whenever their opportunity costs differ, but market equilibrium leaves the division of surplus entirely to relative size.

Part 01 Principles

Comparative advantage

Two economies both gain at any price between their opportunity costs, but nobody gets to choose the price. Relative size sets it, both gain only inside a band exactly as wide as the gap between their costs, and an economy that is better at everything, trading with a partner its own size, can come away with exactly nothing.

Bridge

Comparative advantage took production techniques as fixed endowments across nations. Economic rent investigates where technological differences originate: innovating firms adopt superior techniques to capture temporary rents, which competition systematically erodes while spreading the innovation.

Part 02 Mi2

Economic rent

Seven technologies make the same thing, and the rectangle test every first course teaches leaves five survivors — crowning a sensible middle option that is the cheapest choice at no price at all. Rent is the gap under the price, and competition closes it from below while copying the difference that opened it.

September 2026Read →
Bridge

Economic rent examined how technological differences open profit margins that competition subsequently erodes. Supply and demand investigates the market-level clearing mechanism itself, showing that price-quantity data identifies neither curve without exogenous supply shocks.

Part 03 Mi3

Supply and demand

Price-quantity observations pin the true demand slope only to a bracket between direct and reverse regressions, and the bracket width ratio is exactly 1/R². The goodness-of-fit statistic is the identification failure read backwards: the better a naive line fits the cloud, the less it tells you about the curve.

September 2026Read →
Bridge

Supply and demand showed how shifting curves trace out market equilibria, but took the slopes of those curves as given. Elasticity measures how quantities respond to price changes along any curve, revealing that responsiveness is geometrically identical to the ratio into which any point cuts its own tangent.

Part 04 Mi4

Elasticity

Price elasticity of demand is the ratio of the two segments into which a point divides its own tangent line: lower segment over upper segment. The geometric ruler holds exactly on every smooth demand curve, not just straight lines — revealing why elasticity varies continuously from zero to infinity along a constant slope.

September 2026Read →
Bridge

Elasticity established how responsiveness varies along demand and supply curves. Surplus and efficiency integrates under those curves to measure the gains from trade, proving that non-price rationing inflicts first-order misallocation losses that dwarf the textbook deadweight loss triangle.

Part 05 Mi5

Surplus and efficiency

The textbook deadweight loss triangle scales as the square of the quantity cut, but random rationing under a price ceiling inflicts a first-order misallocation loss. In every linear market, the two channels sum to the quantity cut itself: d² + d(1 − d) = d. A 27% supply restriction destroys exactly 27% of the total gains from trade.

September 2026Read →
Bridge

Surplus and efficiency showed that market clearing maximises total surplus and identified the welfare losses from output cuts. Tax incidence introduces a government wedge between buyer price and seller price, proving that relative elasticities determine who actually bears the burden of the tax.

Part 06 Mi6

Tax incidence

Who writes the cheque changes nothing, to fourteen decimal places; the elasticities set the split exactly for a straight line and only to first order otherwise — and at the revenue-maximising rate the tax has destroyed half the trades and burned half the take.

September 2026Read →
Bridge

Tax incidence and market surplus treated supply curves as fixed marginal cost schedules without asking how firms produce. Cost curves opens the firm's production problem, showing that the long-run cost envelope touches short-run average cost at its minimum at exactly one plant size, and on its slope everywhere else.

Part 07 Mi7

Cost curves

The plant built for an output is run at its own cheapest scale at exactly one output, where the envelope tangency coincides with the short-run minimum. Everywhere else the envelope touches the curve on its slope, and the cost penalty of being forced to operate off-capacity is strictly second-order in the forecast error.

September 2026Read →
Bridge

Cost curves derived the cost envelope for an individual firm with flexible plant scale. Perfect competition aggregates whole numbers of identical firms into an industry, revealing that the integer constraint turns the smooth textbook supply curve into a persistent sawtooth.

Part 08 Mi8

Perfect competition

Free entry cannot drive price down to minimum average cost because firms are discrete whole numbers. Surviving profit is (1 + x)² − 1 fixed costs with x = frac(n̄)/(Bd + n*), giving the long-run supply curve a permanent sawtooth profile where marginal entrants earn strictly positive profits.

September 2026Read →
Bridge

Perfect competition assumed atomistic firms with zero pricing power who take market price as given. Markup and elasticity examines the opposite extreme: a single monopoly seller whose optimal price markup over marginal cost is governed entirely by the price elasticity of demand.

Part 09 Mi9

Markup and elasticity

markup = 1/|elasticity| is exactly true at every optimum — and it is a statement about a single point. Two demand curves through that point set the same price to the last digit, and one passes half a cost increase through while the other passes two and a half times.

September 2026Read →
Bridge

Markup and elasticity analyzed a monopolist sheltered from rival varieties, while perfect competition assumed identical commodities. Monopolistic competition bridges both: differentiated brands compete for market share under free entry, but finite variety leaves firms operating with permanent excess capacity.

Part 10 Mi10

Monopolistic competition

Every textbook result in monopolistic competition is an infinite-variety limit: firm scale falls short of the Dixit–Stiglitz benchmark by exactly 1/n, the equilibrium markup exceeds the continuum markup by 1/(σ(n − 1)), and the market produces (σ − 1)/σ varieties too many.

September 2026Read →
Bridge

Monopolistic competition treated differentiated firms as non-collusive price-setters in a large market. Cartels and the Prisoner's Dilemma examines oligopoly with few players, where firms attempt to coordinate on monopoly output but outsiders capture a disproportionate free-rider windfall.

Part 11 Mi11

Cartels and the Prisoner's Dilemma

A merger of k of n Cournot firms is profitable only when k(n − k + 2)² < (n + 1)²; in a 5-firm market, a 4-firm cartel leaves member profits unchanged to the last cent while the lone outsider quadruples its earnings. Cartel instability is not just secret cheating — it is an overwhelming free-rider windfall.

September 2026Read →
Bridge

Cartels demonstrated how strategic externalities across firms undermine collective discipline in output markets. Tragedy of the commons turns that external wedge onto open-access resources, where individual users equate average product rather than marginal product to cost, rapidly dissipating the shared rent.

Part 12 Mi12

Tragedy of the commons

With n users on an open-access resource, equilibrium equates average product to cost rather than marginal product, dissipating exactly ((n − 1)/n)² of the total resource rent. The second user alone destroys 25% of the maximum surplus, and half of all rent is gone by the fourth user.

September 2026Read →
Bridge

Tragedy of the commons showed how uncoordinated resource use destroys aggregate social rent. Gini and the Lorenz curve concludes Stage 1 Microeconomics by measuring how the resulting income and wealth are distributed across a population, and why a single summary index remains blind to distributional shape.

Part 13 Mi13

Gini and the Lorenz curve

The Gini coefficient measures the average gap between two randomly chosen incomes relative to twice the mean, and 2·AUC − 1 is the exact same geometry applied to a ranking. Two credit scorecards with the identical Gini can differ by 14.4 percentage points of default capture in the top decile.

September 2026Read →
Bridge

Gini and the Lorenz curve closed the microeconomics principles by measuring how income is shared out. Measuring GDP opens the macroeconomics principles by measuring how much there is to share, and shows that the minus sign in front of imports is a correction for foreign content rather than a cost.

Part 14 Ma1

Measuring GDP

GDP is C + I + G + X − M, and the minus sign is a correction, not a cost: an import changes GDP only through the home production it displaces, anywhere from none of its value to all of it, while the net-exports line in the release reads the same across that whole range.

September 2026Read →
Bridge

Measuring GDP added up a year's production in that year's prices. Price indices asks how to compare prices across years, and finds that a fixed basket overstates the rise in the cost of living by an amount that depends on how readily people substitute, which nobody observes.

Part 15 Ma2

Price indices

A fixed basket overstates the cost of living by about half σ times the variance of the price changes: nothing when prices move together, quadratic in how far apart they move. Yet the Fisher index, built only from the two baskets people actually bought, lands on the true answer for every σ at once.

September 2026Read →
Bridge

Price indices showed how a single number can hide the substitution behind it. Unemployment flows does the same for the unemployment rate, a stock fed by people losing jobs and people finding them, whose steady state depends only on the ratio of the two flows.

Part 16 Ma3

Unemployment flows

The unemployment rate depends only on the ratio of job finding to job loss, so doubling layoffs and halving hiring give exactly the same rate, unemployment can nearly double while layoffs fall, and the same 6% can mean two-month spells in one town and eleven-month spells in another.

September 2026Read →
Bridge

Unemployment flows looked at how an economy uses the workers it has from month to month. Compound growth turns to the long run, where a one-point gap in growth rates doubles the gap in living standards in about seventy years, and asks what happens when growth is uncertain.

Part 17 Ma4

Compound growth

A one-point gap in growth rates doubles the gap in living standards in about seventy years. But the average growth rate isn't the rate you grew at, and with uncertain growth the typical path falls behind the expected one for ever: the share of paths above the expected level is exactly Φ(−σ√t/2).

September 2026Read →
Bridge

Compound growth dealt with real output over decades. Money creation turns to the money that output is bought with, following one loan through three banks' balance sheets to show where deposits come from and what the money multiplier really measures.

Part 18 Ma5

Money creation

A bank that lends alone loses (1 − its share) of the loan in reserves, which is why bankers think deposits come first. Banks lending in step lose exactly nothing, so loans create deposits, and the money multiplier is a ratio that holds whichever way the causation runs.

September 2026Read →
Bridge

Money creation closed the macroeconomics principles with the banking system as a whole. Constrained choice opens the intermediate microeconomics by stepping down to a single person's decision, where an indifference curve meets a budget line, and asks what a pay rise does to how much someone chooses to work.

Part 19 U3

Constrained choice

A pay rise makes free time dearer and makes you richer, and every course says theory cannot tell you which effect wins. For the utility function every course draws it does: they cancel to the last digit the arithmetic can carry, at every wage — and what actually shortened the working day was a subsistence floor getting out of the way, not one effect beating the other.

September 2026Read →
Bridge

Constrained choice resolved how a single optimizing individual behaves against fixed constraints. Nash equilibrium expands individual choice into multi-agent strategic interaction, where each player's optimal action depends on the anticipated choices of every rival.

Part 20 U4

Nash equilibrium

One game in eight has no equilibrium you can find by checking cells. In those games your own behaviour is built entirely from the other player's payoffs — so raising a penalty does not reduce the thing it punishes, it buys less monitoring, at exactly the rate that leaves misconduct where it was.

September 2026Read →
Bridge

Nash equilibrium identified static equilibria in simultaneous games. Bargaining and the surplus studies dynamic bilateral negotiation over time, proving that relative patience and outside options dictate a unique division of economic surplus on the Pareto frontier.

Part 21 U5

Bargaining and the surplus

Any point on the frontier beats no deal, and nothing in the picture picks one — until offers alternate, and then the model picks exactly one, built from the other side's impatience over both, with your outside option worth exactly nothing until it crosses the line.

September 2026Read →
Bridge

Bargaining and the surplus showed how two parties who can negotiate directly split the gains from a deal. Externalities brings in a party who is not at the table: when one side's action imposes a cost nobody prices, a bargain, a tax and a quota reach the same quantity and split the money differently, and only the regulator's uncertainty about costs makes the choice between a price and a quantity matter.

Part 22 U10

Externalities

A tax, a quota and a bargain reach the same quantity and move the same money into different pockets, so the choice looks purely distributional — until the regulator is uncertain, and then the expected-loss ratio between price and quantity is the square of a slope ratio, crossing at exactly one.

September 2026Read →
Bridge

Externalities assumed the costly action could be seen and measured, so a bargain, a tax or a quota could aim straight at it. Efficiency wages turns to an action nobody can see, a worker's effort: when effort cannot be observed, employers pay above the market-clearing wage so that losing the job hurts, which makes involuntary unemployment an equilibrium outcome.

Part 23 U6

Efficiency wages

The wage buys effort, and the cheapest effort sits where the effort elasticity is exactly one — a number labour demand never enters, which is why one worker and a thousand draw the same pay and the queue outside is the collateral, not the failure.
